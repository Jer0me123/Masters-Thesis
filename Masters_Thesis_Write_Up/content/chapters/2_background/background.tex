\section{Re-LAION-5B Dataset}
\label{sec:relaion5b}

Re-LAION-5B is a large-scale, openly released image-text dataset comprising approximately
5.85 billion image-text pairs harvested from publicly accessible web content via Common
Crawl \cite{Schuhmann_2022_Laion5b, CommonCrawl_2007_CommonCrawl}. It is a re-release of
the original LAION-5B, motivated by the discovery of \acrshort{csam} within the original
corpus. The re-release applies improved safety filtering pipelines, including enhanced
perceptual hashing and classifier-based detection, to remove harmful content
\cite{Laion_2024_Relaion5B}.

The construction pipeline parses HTML pages indexed by Common Crawl to extract image URLs
paired with alt text. These pairs are filtered using cosine similarity between image and
text embeddings computed with OpenAI's ViT-B/32 CLIP model for English pairs and a
multilingual CLIP variant for all others \cite{Radford_2021_CLIP}, retaining pairs above
0.28 for English content and 0.26 for non-English content, a step that removes
approximately 90\% of the original 50 billion candidates \cite{Schuhmann_2022_Laion5b}.
Additional stages remove near-duplicate pairs, apply NSFW classifiers, and enforce minimum
resolution and caption length requirements. The resulting dataset is stratified into three
subsets summarised in Table~\ref{tab:laion_subsets}. The LAION-1B-nolang subset frequently
contains images of products, places, or content dominated by proper nouns, for which no
language could be reliably detected.

LAION-Aesthetics v2 5+ is a quality-filtered offshoot of LAION-2B-en rather than a
separate corpus, constructed using a linear classifier trained on CLIP ViT-L/14 embeddings
to predict human aesthetic ratings \cite{Schuhmann_2022_LaionAesthetics}. It serves as the
training corpus for Stable Diffusion v1.5.

Despite its scale, Re-LAION-5B carries significant concerns. Birhane et
al.~\cite{Birhane_2021_MultiModalDatasetsMisogynyPornography} audited LAION-400M, a
predecessor, and identified substantial quantities of misogynistic, pornographic, and
racially derogatory content, confirming that automated filtering is insufficient at
billion-scale. Beyond explicit harmful content, the web-scraped construction process
introduces systematic demographic imbalances, as the statistical composition of online
imagery reflects societal inequities in content production and visual representation.

A practically significant limitation of the Re-LAION-5B release is the absence of
pre-computed embeddings. The original LAION-5B included pre-computed CLIP embeddings
enabling efficient nearest-neighbour retrieval. In the re-release these were not made
available, as the re-filtering altered the dataset composition and the embedding
infrastructure was not reconstructed \cite{Laion_2024_Relaion5B}. Any embedding-based
retrieval therefore requires independently computing CLIP embeddings from raw image
downloads, introducing substantial overhead. For this thesis, this absence necessitates
the derivation of embeddings as part of the data acquisition pipeline.

A further consequence of Re-LAION-5B's web-sourced construction is link attrition: the
proportion of URLs that resolve successfully degrades over time as hosting services expire
or content is removed \cite{Klein_2014_LinkRot}. Because Re-LAION-5B re-filters URLs
originally crawled by Common Crawl, the gap between crawl date and download date introduces
a decay component that cannot be precisely quantified without access to per-URL crawl
timestamps. The surviving image pool is therefore not a random sample of the original
corpus but a temporally filtered subset shaped by domain-specific hosting practices and
content removal patterns. Additionally, web-scraped datasets exhibit systematic
domain concentration, whereby a disproportionate share of images originates from a small
number of large hosting platforms. Birhane et
al.~\cite{Birhane_2021_MultiModalDatasetsMisogynyPornography} demonstrate that such
structural biases propagate directly into the resulting dataset, and Zeng et
al.~\cite{Zeng_2024_UnderstandingBiasinLargeScaleVisualDatasets} similarly show that
large-scale web-scraped datasets are reliably distinguishable by neural networks based on
their dataset-specific content distributions.

\section{Stable Diffusion: Architecture, Training, and Dataset Lineage}
\label{sec:stable_diffusion}

Diffusion models offer several advantages over alternative generative architectures.
Unlike GANs \cite{Goodfellow_2014_GAN}, they avoid mode collapse and training instability
\cite{Dhariwal_2021_DiffusionBeatGANs}, and they bypass the surrogate loss limitation of
VAEs \cite{Kingma_2013_VAE} that constrains reconstruction fidelity. These properties have
established diffusion models as the dominant architecture for high-quality image synthesis.

Stable Diffusion is a family of open-source text-to-image models based on the Latent
Diffusion Model (LDM) framework of Rombach et al.~\cite{Rombach_2022_CVPR}. The central
innovation is performing the generative process in a compressed latent space rather than
at full pixel resolution. A Variational Autoencoder \cite{Kingma_2013_VAE} is trained to
map high-resolution inputs into a lower-dimensional latent space; once trained, its
encoder and decoder are frozen and all generative modelling is performed within this
latent space. This compression dramatically reduces the computational cost relative to
pixel-space diffusion models such as DALL-E \cite{Ramesh_2021_DALLE}.

The diffusion process follows the DDPM framework \cite{Ho_2020_DDPM}, in which a forward
process corrupts a clean latent by adding Gaussian noise over $T$ timesteps until it
approximates pure Gaussian noise. A learned reverse process, implemented as a U-Net
\cite{Ronneberger_2015_UNet}, iteratively denoises a noisy latent to recover a clean
sample. Text conditioning is achieved via cross-attention in the U-Net's intermediate
layers, where a pretrained CLIP text encoder \cite{Radford_2021_CLIP} produces embeddings
injected at multiple spatial resolutions.

\subsection*{Classifier-Free Guidance}
\label{subsec:cfg}

Classifier-Free Guidance (CFG) \cite{Ho_2022_CFG} is applied during inference to
strengthen the influence of text conditioning. A single model is jointly trained as both
a conditional and an unconditional model, with the text conditioning dropped with a
probability of 10\% during training in the case of Stable Diffusion v1.5
\cite{StableDiffusion_2022_SD15modelcard}. At inference, two forward passes are performed
per denoising step and the final noise estimate is computed as:

\begin{equation}
	\tilde{\epsilon}_\theta(z_t, c) = \epsilon_\theta(z_t, \emptyset)
	+ s \cdot \bigl(\epsilon_\theta(z_t, c) -
	\epsilon_\theta(z_t, \emptyset)\bigr)
\end{equation}

where $z_t$ is the noisy latent at timestep $t$, $c$ is the text conditioning,
$\emptyset$ denotes the null unconditional token, and $s \geq 1$ is the guidance scale.
Higher guidance scales produce outputs more strongly adherent to the prompt at the cost of
stochastic diversity. Critically for bias analysis, higher CFG scales push the model more
strongly toward its learned conditional distribution, amplifying demographic stereotypes
and spurious correlations present in the training data.

\subsection*{Training Lineage}
\label{subsec:training_lineage}

Stable Diffusion v1.5 was released by RunwayML as a continuation of training from v1.2,
itself initialised from v1.1 weights produced by CompVis
\cite{StableDiffusion_2022_SD15modelcard}. Training was conducted on LAION-Aesthetics v2
5+ using the AdamW optimiser with a batch size of 2048 at 512$\times$512 pixel
resolution. The training corpus is a direct derivative of LAION-2B-en, inheriting its
web-scraped composition, demographic imbalances, and the spurious statistical regularities
documented in Section~\ref{sec:relaion5b}. The open-source availability of both model
weights and documented training data lineage makes Stable Diffusion v1.5 particularly
well-suited for mechanistic bias analysis, as findings can be independently verified and
directly linked to known properties of the training corpus. Later models in the Stable
Diffusion family, including SDXL \cite{Podell_2023_SDXL} and Stable Diffusion 3
\cite{Esser_2024_SD3}, rely on proprietary training corpora whose composition has not been
disclosed, substantially limiting the capacity for independent bias auditing and
reinforcing the methodological value of focusing on v1.5.

\subsection{Low-Rank Adaptation of Diffusion Models}
\label{subsec:lora}

Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) \cite{Hu_2021_LoRA} is a parameter-efficient fine-tuning
technique in which the weight update $\Delta W$ for a matrix $W \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times
	k}$ is decomposed into two low-rank matrices $A \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times r}$ and $B \in
\mathbb{R}^{r \times k}$, where $r \ll \min(d, k)$:

\[
W' = W + \Delta W = W + AB
\]

Because only $A$ and $B$ are trained while $W$ is frozen, the number of trainable
parameters is reduced from $d \times k$ to $r(d + k)$. LoRA adapters can be fused
directly into base weights at inference time, eliminating runtime overhead.

Latent Consistency Models (LCMs) \cite{Song_2023_LCM} introduce a distillation-based
objective enabling high-quality generation in two to four denoising steps rather than the
twenty to fifty required by standard DDPM samplers. LCM-LoRA \cite{Luo_2023_LCMLoRA}
applies this distillation as a LoRA adapter rather than requiring full model retraining,
implementing a consistency function that maps predictions at different noise levels to the
same clean image, allowing the sampling trajectory to skip intermediate timesteps.

Critically, LCM-LoRA's training objective is purely geometric: the adapter is optimised
to reproduce the output distribution of the base model more efficiently, with no training
signal derived from demographic content, aesthetic labels, or semantic categories. The
LCM distillation loss operates on the consistency of latent-space trajectories across
noise levels, ensuring that predictions at different timesteps map to the same clean
image. The adapter weights therefore encode information about the structure of the
denoising process rather than the content of the training images. This distinguishes
speed-based LoRAs categorically from concept LoRAs or style LoRAs, which are explicitly
trained to shift the model's semantic prior toward a specific concept or appearance, and
which consequently alter what the model generates. Consequently, applying LCM-LoRA to
Stable Diffusion v1.5 does not introduce additional demographic signal and is therefore
unlikely to confound measurements of the base model's demographic prior, making it
suitable for use in the generation pipeline of this thesis.

\subsection{Aesthetic Quality Assessment in Large-Scale Image Datasets}
\label{subsec:aesthetic_scoring}

Aesthetic quality in web-scraped datasets varies enormously. To address this, Schuhmann
\cite{Schuhmann_2022_LaionAesthetics} trained a linear predictor on CLIP ViT-L/14
embeddings using human preference ratings from the Simulacra Aesthetic Captions (SAC)
dataset \cite{Pressman_2022_SAC}, producing a lightweight scalar aesthetic score. This
predictor was used to construct the LAION-Aesthetics v2 subsets, with the 5+ threshold
retaining images above a score of 5.0 out of 10.

In the context of evaluating generative outputs, aesthetic scoring serves a different
purpose. Diffusion models operating at reduced step counts can produce structurally
coherent images that nonetheless exhibit quality failures such as posterisation or large
undifferentiated regions from incomplete denoising, failures not captured by detection-based
validation. A conservative threshold of 3.0 is adopted in this work as a minimum
viability gate, positioned well below the training cutoff to avoid introducing systematic
upward quality bias into the generated subset while still rejecting genuinely corrupted
outputs.

\subsection{Face Detection Models and Evaluation Benchmarks}
\label{subsec:face_detection}

Face detection produces axis-aligned bounding boxes with confidence scores for human faces
within an image. Modern detectors are predominantly single-stage architectures that perform
classification and regression jointly, offering substantially higher throughput than
two-stage approaches \cite{Deng_2020_RetinaFace}. The WIDER Face benchmark
\cite{Yang_2016_WIDERFace} is the standard evaluation dataset, comprising 32,203 images
with 393,703 annotated faces across three difficulty tiers based on face size and
occlusion. For applications requiring detection of clearly visible, unoccluded faces in
generative or retrieval settings, Easy and Medium Average Precision are the more relevant
indicators, as the Hard partition targets conditions unlikely to arise in such contexts.

RetinaFace \cite{Deng_2020_RetinaFace} established a strong baseline through joint
detection and five-point landmark regression within a Feature Pyramid Network. SCRFD
\cite{Guo_2021_SCRFD} introduced sample and computation redistribution to produce a family
of efficient detectors at reduced FLOPs. YOLOv8-face adapts the YOLOv8 architecture
\cite{Jocher_2023_YOLOv8} to face detection, offering competitive accuracy on Easy and
Medium partitions with substantially higher throughput, making it well-suited for
large-scale filtering pipelines where speed is a primary constraint.

For this work, the relevant properties are single-face precision and inference throughput,
since structural filtering is applied to hundreds of thousands of candidates. Small-face
recall is a secondary concern given that the structural validation criteria require face
regions of sufficient size to support downstream FaceMesh landmark extraction.

\subsection{Facial Landmark Estimation: MediaPipe FaceMesh}
\label{subsec:facemesh}

Facial landmark estimation recovers the positions of anatomically defined keypoints within
a detected face region. MediaPipe FaceMesh \cite{Kartynnik_2019_FaceMesh} predicts 468
three-dimensional face landmarks from a single RGB image using a lightweight convolutional
network, producing dense coverage across the full facial surface including eye contours,
eyebrows, lips, nose, and outer face boundary.

FaceMesh serves a functionally distinct role from face detection in structural validation.
Detection confirms the presence and approximate location of a face; landmark estimation
confirms that the detected region contains a geometrically coherent face with sufficient
surface detail for downstream processing. A detected bounding box may enclose a face-like
region that is too blurred, too occluded, or too poorly rendered to yield reliable
landmarks, and FaceMesh failure on such regions provides a principled rejection criterion.
The convex hull of the predicted landmarks also provides a precise polygonal mask of the
facial skin region, enabling tightly cropped face extractions that exclude surrounding
hair, clothing, or background, a property that is directly relevant when the extracted
region is used for skin tone classification where non-skin pixels would introduce spurious
colour signal.

This functional complementarity is the basis for the two-stage face processing pipeline
adopted in this work: YOLO-based detection identifies candidate face regions efficiently
at scale, and FaceMesh subsequently validates geometric coherence and produces the
segmented face crop used in downstream demographic annotation.

\section{Similarity Search in High-Dimensional Embedding Spaces}
\label{sec:faiss}

CLIP embeddings \cite{Radford_2021_CLIP} are typically 512- or 768-dimensional
floating-point vectors, and retrieval is framed as a nearest-neighbour search in this
shared space. When similarity is measured by cosine similarity, vectors are L2-normalised
prior to indexing and inner product search is performed, since for unit vectors the two
are equivalent.

Naive exact search scales as $O(Nd)$, requiring $N \times d$ operations per query, which
becomes computationally intractable for databases containing hundreds of millions to
billions of entries. Efficient similarity search therefore requires indexing structures
that reduce the number of comparisons per query.

\subsection*{The FAISS Library}
\label{subsec:faiss_library}

The Facebook AI Similarity Search (FAISS) library \cite{Douze_2024_FAISS} is a widely
adopted open-source library providing indexing structures and algorithms for similarity
search and clustering of dense vectors. It supports both CPU and GPU implementations and
spans a spectrum from exact to approximate nearest-neighbour search.

\subsection*{Exact Search: IndexFlatIP}
\label{subsec:indexflatip}

The simplest index, IndexFlatIP, performs exact inner product search with $O(Nd)$
complexity, exhaustively computing the inner product between a query and every indexed
vector. No approximation is applied, guaranteeing that true nearest neighbours are
returned for every query. Despite being exhaustive, it substantially outperforms
unoptimised linear scan by exploiting BLAS-level matrix operations and CPU SIMD
acceleration.

\subsection*{Approximate Search Methods}
\label{subsec:ann_methods}

For databases where exact search is too slow, FAISS provides approximate nearest-neighbour
index types that trade retrieval exactness for substantially reduced query times.
IndexIVFFlat partitions the embedding space into $k$ Voronoi cells using k-means
clustering and at query time searches only the $n_\text{probe}$ nearest cells, reducing
complexity to approximately $O(n_\text{probe} \cdot N/k)$ \cite{Jegou_2011_PQ}.
IndexHNSWFlat organises indexed vectors into a Hierarchical Navigable Small World graph
\cite{Malkov_2020_HNSW}, yielding empirically logarithmic query complexity $O(\log N)$
with strong recall and low memory overhead. IndexIVFPQ combines inverted file partitioning
with Product Quantisation \cite{Jegou_2011_PQ}, compressing vectors into quantised
subvectors to enable billion-scale retrieval on commodity hardware at the cost of
quantisation error, which corrupts similarity scores relative to true distances.

Approximate search methods additionally introduce search error, the failure to visit index
regions containing true nearest neighbours during traversal. For applications where
retrieval correctness is critical, such as when retrieved items form the empirical basis of
a downstream analysis, exact search with IndexFlatIP eliminates both error sources by
guaranteeing complete traversal with no vector compression, and is therefore adopted in
this work to ensure that retrieval-induced approximation error does not confound downstream
bias measurements.

\section{Spurious Features in Machine Learning}
\label{sec:spurious_features}

\subsection*{Defining Spurious Features and Spurious Correlations}
\label{subsec:spurious_definitions}

A key challenge in deploying machine learning models reliably is their tendency to learn
features that are predictive within the training data but carry no causal or semantic
relationship to the target variable. Such features are termed spurious features, and the
statistical associations they form with target labels are known as spurious correlations
\cite{Arjovsky_2020_InvariantRiskMinimization,
	Sagawa_2020_DistributionallyRobustNeuralNetworksForGroupShifts}. Formally, a spurious
correlation exists when a feature $f$ co-occurs with a label $y$ in the training
distribution but this co-occurrence does not persist under distribution shift, i.e.,
$p_{\text{train}}(f \mid y) \neq p_{\text{test}}(f \mid y)$, or when $f$ carries no
causal information about $y$ despite being statistically correlated with it. A model that
exploits such a feature in lieu of causally relevant ones is said to learn a shortcut
\cite{Geirhos_2020_ShortcutLearningInDeepNeuralNetworks}.

Geirhos et al.~\cite{Geirhos_2020_ShortcutLearningInDeepNeuralNetworks} characterise a
shortcut as any decision rule that performs well on the training distribution by exploiting
a feature correlated with the correct label but which does not reflect the underlying task
concept. Critically, they argue that shortcut learning is not a pathological edge case but
a rational consequence of empirical risk minimisation: gradient-based optimisation will
consistently favour whichever feature reduces training loss most efficiently.

\subsection*{Taxonomies of Spurious Features in Computer Vision}
\label{subsec:spurious_taxonomy}

Spurious features in visual data can be broadly categorised by the level of the image
hierarchy at which they reside and whether they reflect properties of the subject, the
context, or the acquisition process.

At the pixel and statistical level, low-level image attributes including mean colour
channel values, brightness distributions, contrast statistics, and jpeg compression
artefacts can serve as reliable proxies for class labels when the dataset collection
process introduces systematic correlations between acquisition conditions and class
membership. Jo and Bengio~\cite{Jo_2017_SurfaceStatisticalRegularities} demonstrate that
CNNs trained on benchmark datasets are inclined to learn surface statistical regularities
rather than high-level abstractions, achieving high accuracy even when these regularities
bear no semantic relationship to the class concept. Geirhos et
al.~\cite{Geirhos_2019_TextureBias} further demonstrate that ImageNet-trained
\acrshort{cnn}s exhibit a strong texture bias, relying on texture for object
classification rather than shape.

At the frequency domain level, Wang et
al.~\cite{Wang_2020_HighFrequencyComponentHelpsExplainTheGeneralizationOfCNN} demonstrate
that \acrshort{cnn}s can exploit high-frequency features imperceptible to the human eye as
spurious shortcuts, and that adversarially introduced high-frequency perturbations can
substantially alter model decisions without any visible change to image content. This
sensitivity is particularly concerning in web-sourced datasets where different image
origins carry systematically different frequency signatures arising from variation in
camera hardware, processing pipelines, and compression settings.

At the contextual and background level, spurious features can arise from statistical
regularities in scene composition rather than in the subject itself. Beery et
al.~\cite{Beery_2018_RecognitionTerraIncognita} show that classifiers trained on images
where animals consistently appear against certain background types fail severely when the
same animal appears against an unfamiliar background. Xiao et
al.~\cite{Xiao_2021_NoiseOrSignal} demonstrate that models achieve non-trivial
classification accuracy when trained on background-only images with foreground objects
masked, establishing that contextual scene statistics constitute a pervasive spurious
signal. Torralba and Efros~\cite{Torralba_2011_UnbiasedLookDatasetBias} show that models
trained on one visual dataset and evaluated on another exhibit sharp performance drops,
demonstrating that much of what models learn reflects dataset-specific regularities rather
than generalisable visual concepts.

At the semantic and co-occurrence level, spurious correlations can be encoded in
higher-level statistical regularities such as the tendency for certain object categories
to co-occur with certain scenes, or for certain demographic attributes to be associated
with particular activities. Zhao et al.~\cite{Zhao_2017_MenAlsoLikeShopping} demonstrate
that gender-stereotypical object co-occurrences in captioning datasets cause models to
amplify those associations in their outputs. Singh et
al.~\cite{Singh_2020_DontJudgeObjectContext} further show that performance degrades
substantially when objects appear without their typical co-occurring context, confirming
that semantic co-occurrence regularities function as learned shortcuts.

At the structural and geometric level, classical edge detection algorithms such as the
Canny edge detector \cite{Canny_1986_CannyEdgeDetection} reduce an image to a binary
boundary map capturing object contours while discarding colour and texture. Class-agnostic
segmentation tools such as the Segment Anything Model \cite{Kirillov_2023_SAM} extend
this to higher-quality contour representations. Monocular depth estimation encodes the
relative spatial geometry of a scene without reference to object semantics. Since these
structural properties are systematically shaped by the contexts in which images are
captured, they can carry dataset-specific or demographic-specific spurious signals
invisible to pixel-level analysis. Brendel and Bethge~\cite{Brendel_2019_BagNet}
demonstrate that models operating solely on small independent image patches achieve
surprisingly high accuracy on ImageNet, confirming that local structural features within
small receptive fields are sufficient to support classification without any holistic
understanding of image content.

At the person level, a person's appearance, shape, spatial location, and pose may each
independently correlate with demographic group membership when the collection conditions
of a dataset introduce systematic differences in how different groups are photographed.
This has direct consequences for fairness-through-blindness approaches, which attempt to
remove protected attribute information by occluding the person in an image
\cite{Burns_2018_WomenAlsoSnowboard, Wang_2019_BalancedDatasetsNotEnough}. Because
person-level spurious features are intertwined with background signals and encoded across
multiple representational layers simultaneously, such occlusion-based interventions may
leave substantial demographic information intact, a limitation examined in detail in
Section~\ref{subsec:meister2023}.

At the semantic representation level, richer image representations that preserve object
identity while discarding pixel-level detail can themselves carry dataset-specific spurious
signals. Semantic segmentation maps retain the spatial distribution of scene categories
while removing texture and colour. Object detection representations preserve object
co-occurrence statistics while discarding internal pixel content. Image captions discard
all visual information in favour of natural language but still encode semantic regularities
reflecting the statistical composition of the underlying image distribution. VAE
reconstructions, as implemented in latent diffusion architectures \cite{Rombach_2022_CVPR},
provide a further diagnostic: if reconstructed images remain discriminable after
compression, the bias resides primarily in semantic content rather than low-level
acquisition artefacts. Taken together, these representation types demonstrate that spurious
features are not confined to the pixel domain but permeate the full hierarchy of visual
abstraction.

\subsection*{Spurious Features and Demographic Bias}
\label{subsec:spurious_demographic}

When a spurious feature correlates with a sensitive attribute such as gender, race, or
skin tone, a model that exploits the shortcut will systematically produce disparate
outcomes across demographic groups. Buolamwini and
Gebru~\cite{Buolamwini_2018_GenderShades} provide a foundational empirical
demonstration: commercial gender classifiers trained on datasets overrepresenting
lighter-skinned individuals exhibit substantially higher error rates for darker-skinned
individuals, attributable in part to the model relying on skin-tone correlates as
classification shortcuts. Mehrabi et
al.~\cite{Mehrabi_2022_SurveyBiasFairnessMachine} catalogue the mechanisms by which
demographic shortcuts enter models, including representation bias (underrepresentation of
certain groups in training data), measurement bias (features measured differently across
groups), and aggregation bias (heterogeneous sub-populations pooled under a single model).

Critically, features that appear neutral, including colour statistics, pose distributions,
and background scenes, can function as proxies for demographic attributes when dataset
composition creates systematic correlations between low-level properties and group
membership. Meister et al.~\cite{Meister_2023_GenderArtifactsinVisualDatasets} term these
gender artifacts and demonstrate that they are effectively impossible to fully remove from
standard visual datasets without also removing information necessary for downstream tasks.
This proxy mechanism means that debiasing interventions which remove explicit demographic
signals may leave intact the spurious low-level features that encode equivalent
information, producing only superficial corrections.

\subsection*{Spurious Features in Text-to-Image Generative Systems}
\label{subsec:spurious_generative}

The concern about spurious features takes on a distinct character in text-to-image
generative models. Whereas discriminative models learn to exploit spurious correlations to
make predictions, generative models trained on biased data learn to reproduce those
correlations in their outputs: biased statistical regularities present in the training
corpus become embedded in the model's learned prior over images, causing the model to
synthesise spurious feature patterns even when the conditioning prompt contains no
information that should elicit them.

The training pipeline of latent diffusion models such as Stable Diffusion
\cite{Rombach_2022_CVPR} illustrates this concretely. The model learns a joint distribution
over image latents conditioned on text embeddings derived from CLIP
\cite{Radford_2021_CLIP}. Because CLIP is itself trained on web-scale image-text data, it
encodes the same compositional and societal biases present in internet imagery
\cite{Birhane_2021_MultiModalDatasetsMisogynyPornography, Schuhmann_2022_Laion5b}, and
these biases are subsequently inherited by diffusion models conditioned on its embeddings.
Spurious correlations in the training data between text tokens and low-level image features
are absorbed into the diffusion model's score function, causing those features to be
hallucinated at generation time even in the absence of explicit demographic prompting.

Bianchi et al.~\cite{Bianchi_2023_TTIGenAmplifiesDemographicStereotypes} demonstrate this
empirically, showing that text-to-image models not only reflect demographic
under-representation present in their training data but actively amplify stereotypical
associations: occupation prompts produce generated images with demographic distributions
more extreme than those in real-world labour statistics. Luccioni et
al.~\cite{Luccioni_2023_AnalyzingSocietalRepresentationsDiffusionModels} extend this
finding across a broader set of models, confirming that the amplification effect is
systemic. Critically, neither study addresses which specific low-level image features
encode these biases, treating the model as a black box and measuring only demographic
outcomes.

\subsection*{Understanding Bias in Large-Scale Visual Datasets}
\label{subsec:zeng2024}

The taxonomy above provides a conceptual decomposition of spurious features across
different levels of image representation, but does not quantify their relative contribution
to dataset-level bias. Zeng et al.~\cite{Zeng_2024_UnderstandingBiasinLargeScaleVisualDatasets}
operationalise this through a transformation-based framework, aiming to identify the
concrete forms of bias that make large-scale pre-training datasets distinguishable by
neural networks. Their work builds on the dataset classification task introduced by
Torralba and Efros~\cite{Torralba_2011_UnbiasedLookDatasetBias} and revisited by Liu and
He~\cite{Liu_2024_DecadeBattleDatasetBias}, who found that three widely-used datasets,
YFCC100M \cite{Thomee_2016_YFCC100M}, CC12M \cite{Changpinyo_2021_CC12M}, and
DataComp-1B \cite{Gadre_2023_DataComp}, collectively referred to as YCD, can be classified
with 84.7\% accuracy. While this confirms the datasets are biased, it does not explain
which image properties are responsible. Zeng et al.\ address this gap through a
transformation-based framework.

\subsubsection*{Transformation-Based Bias Quantification}
\label{subsubsec:zeng_transformations}

The central contribution is a transformation-based framework: apply a transformation that
preserves one type of visual information while removing all others, train a dataset
classifier on the transformed images, and use the resulting accuracy to measure how much
bias that type of information carries. For each transformation, 1M images per dataset are
used for training and 10K for validation using a ConvNeXt-Tiny model trained for 30
epochs. On unmodified images, a reference accuracy of 82.0\% is achieved.

At the semantic level, segmentation using ViT-Adapter-Large with the ADE20K vocabulary
achieves 69.8\%; object detection using ViTDet-Huge with LVIS achieves 61.9\%; and image
captioning with LLaVA 1.5 achieves 63.8\% for short captions and 66.1\% for long captions.
These results show that semantic content, specifically which categories of objects appear in
each dataset, is a primary source of dataset bias.

The structural transformations produce a striking result: Canny edge detection achieves
71.0\%, SAM-based contour extraction reaches 73.2\%, and monocular depth estimation with
Depth-Anything-V2 yields 73.1\%. These representations contain no object labels, yet they
outperform most semantic transformations, showing that the shapes and spatial arrangements
of objects differ significantly across datasets and carry as much discriminative information
as object category.

Pixel and patch shuffling experiments separate local structure from global spatial layout.
Randomly shuffling individual pixels removes all spatial structure, reducing the
classifier to colour statistics alone; accuracy falls to 52.2\%, just above the 33.3\%
chance level. Shuffling 16$\times$16 patches instead recovers nearly all discriminability
at 81.2\%. This shows that local spatial structure within small image regions is
sufficient to distinguish datasets, and that global composition matters much less than
local texture and shape. A mean-RGB experiment compressing each image to a single average
colour per channel achieves 48.5\%, roughly 15 percentage points above chance, suggesting
that even the global colour palette of images differs systematically across datasets.
Frequency domain analysis shows high-pass filters yield 79.2\% and low-pass filters yield
70.4\%, indicating that spurious signals exist across the full frequency spectrum and
cannot be eliminated by targeting a single frequency range.

A VAE experiment provides further context: passing images through the bottleneck of a
pretrained Stable Diffusion VAE reduces accuracy from 82.0\% to 77.4\%, showing that
low-level properties such as jpeg compression artefacts contribute relatively little to
the overall bias compared to semantic and structural content. Finally, combining two
transformations together increases accuracy beyond what either achieves alone, for example
combining pixel shuffling with SAM contours reaches 74.2\% compared to 73.1\% for SAM
contours alone, demonstrating that different types of spurious signals carry partly
independent information. This finding is directly relevant to the multi-transformation
approach used in this thesis.

\subsubsection*{Propagation of Bias into Generative Models}
\label{subsubsec:zeng_generative}

A central question for this thesis is whether biases in training data carry through into
generative model outputs. Zeng et al.\ test this by training separate unconditional
Diffusion Transformer (DiT) models on each of the YCD datasets and classifying the
resulting synthetic images. These generated images can be classified with 77.6\% accuracy,
nearly matching the 82.0\% reference on real images, demonstrating that generative models
reproduce the distributional biases of their training data to a degree largely preserved
in their outputs. A second experiment using a single pretrained SDXL-Turbo model
conditioned on LLaVA-generated captions achieves 58.1\%, confirming that semantic bias is
partly transmitted even when the generative model is not dataset-specific. Together, these
results show a clear link between training data composition and generative output,
directly supporting the central hypothesis of this thesis.

This distinction establishes a two-stage bias pipeline that is central to the present
work: biases originate in the training dataset and are subsequently encoded into the
generative model, where they may be preserved or amplified during synthesis. Re-LAION-5B
represents the source stage of this pipeline, and Stable Diffusion v1.5 represents the
generative stage, making the relationship between their respective spurious feature
distributions a primary object of analysis.

\subsubsection*{Semantic Explanation of Dataset Differences}
\label{subsubsec:zeng_semantic}

To explain why the datasets are distinguishable, Zeng et al.\ apply \acrshort{gradcam} to the
reference classifier, showing that the model focuses on specific objects to identify
dataset origin. A logistic regression model trained on binary object-presence vectors
achieves 52.0--52.4\% accuracy depending on vocabulary, well above chance but well below
the 82.0\% of the neural classifier, showing that object identity alone does not fully
explain discriminability and that lower-level structural signals also play a role.
Open-ended language analysis using LDA topic modelling characterises YFCC as outdoor and
human-centred, DataComp as product-focused with clean backgrounds, and CC as a mix of the
two.

These findings directly motivate the probe design adopted in this thesis, where
transformations are selected to isolate semantic, structural, and low-level statistical
components of the image. By evaluating classification performance under each
transformation, the contribution of each feature type to dataset-specific bias can be
quantified independently. When these spurious features correlate with demographic
attributes, they provide a mechanism through which demographic information can be inferred
from non-semantic image properties. The probe-based framework therefore enables not only
the detection of dataset bias, but the identification of feature types that act as proxies
for demographic attributes.

\subsection*{Evaluation of Image Captioning Quality}
\label{subsec:captioning_metrics}

The transformation-based framework of Zeng et al.\ requires that generated captions
faithfully reflect image content, as a captioning model producing degenerate descriptions
would corrupt the semantic signal the transformation is designed to preserve. Caption-based
representations discard all pixel-level information, isolating semantic content entirely.
If dataset classification remains above chance under this transformation, the evidence
strongly suggests that the discriminative signal resides in systematic differences in
object co-occurrence, scene composition, or semantic structure rather than in low-level
image statistics. Selecting an appropriate captioning model therefore requires a
principled evaluation protocol. Reference-based n-gram metrics such as BLEU
\cite{Papineni_2002_BLEU}, METEOR \cite{Banerjee_2005_METEOR}, and CIDEr
\cite{Vedantam_2015_CIDEr} assess surface-level lexical overlap with human-authored
references, while diversity metrics \cite{Li_2016_DiversityNMT} detect template collapse
invisible to precision measures. At the semantic level, Sentence-BERT
\cite{Reimers_2019_SBERT} provides a paraphrase-robust measure of meaning preservation,
and CLIPScore \cite{Hessel_2021_CLIPScore} offers reference-free visual grounding by
computing cosine similarity between CLIP image and text embeddings. RefCLIP-S extends this
as the harmonic mean of CLIPScore and maximum reference similarity, achieving the highest
correlation with human judgements of any single metric. No single metric is sufficient,
and the failure modes of each motivate the multi-metric evaluation protocol employed in
this thesis.

\subsection*{Gender Artifacts in Visual Datasets}
\label{subsec:meister2023}

Meister et al.~\cite{Meister_2023_GenderArtifactsinVisualDatasets} systematically
investigate the nature and prevalence of gender artifacts in large-scale computer vision
datasets. A gender artifact is defined as a visual cue that is both learnable by a modern
image classifier and interpretable, meaning it has a recognisable human corollary such as
colour, shape, or scene composition. The work is motivated by a gap in the fairness
literature: fairness-through-blindness techniques assume that removing a person's visible
appearance from an image is sufficient to eliminate gendered signal. Meister et al.\
challenge this by asking to what extent gendered information can truly be removed from a
visual dataset. The gender artifact model is treated as a diagnostic tool for understanding
dataset correlations, not a system for predicting gender identity; the gender labels used
are derived from external annotator perception and are used solely to reveal distributional
differences in the data.

\subsubsection*{Datasets and Model}
\label{subsubsec:meister_datasets}

The analysis focuses on MS-COCO \cite{Lin_2014_COCO} and
OpenImages \cite{Kuznetsova_2020_OpenImagesV4}. Gender labels for COCO are inferred from
captions via keyword matching \cite{Zhao_2017_MenAlsoLikeShopping}; for OpenImages they
come from the MIAP subset \cite{Schumann_2021_StepInclusivePeopleAnnotations}. Both
datasets are skewed male at 69.2\% and 62.9\% respectively. The gender artifact model is a
ResNet-50 \cite{He_2016_DeepResidualLearning} pretrained on ImageNet, with performance
reported as AUC where 50\% represents chance-level discrimination.

\subsubsection*{Resolution and Colour Artifacts}
\label{subsubsec:meister_colour}

Images downsampled to 7$\times$7 pixels, at which almost all semantic content is
destroyed, yield an AUC of 59.2\% on COCO and 63.9\% on OpenImages in colour. Repeating
the experiment in greyscale, the AUC falls to near chance at 28$\times$28, showing that
colour is the primary carrier of gender-correlated signal at low resolutions. A logistic
regression classifier trained on just the three mean RGB channel values achieves 58.0\%
and 59.1\% on COCO and OpenImages respectively, likely due to a compositional confound:
male-labelled images are disproportionately set in outdoor sports contexts dominated by
green tones, while female-labelled images more often feature close-up portraits with
lighter average pixel values.

\subsubsection*{Person and Background Artifacts}
\label{subsubsec:meister_person}

A systematic occlusion protocol separates gender artifacts arising from the person from
those arising from the background. With all person pixels visible, the model achieves
93.4\% AUC on COCO. Removing the background reduces this only slightly to 92.7\%,
confirming person appearance as the dominant signal under normal conditions.

When the person's appearance is replaced by a white segmentation mask but the background
remains visible, the model still achieves 79.6\% AUC, showing that the silhouette alone
carries substantial gendered information. Removing the background as well reduces
performance to 74.8\%, confirming that silhouette shape and position are meaningful gender
artifacts independent of scene context. Training on the 17 COCO keypoint locations alone
yields 64.8\%, and a normalised pose-only representation still achieves 58.0\%, establishing
body pose as an independent gender artifact.

Under the MaskRect condition, which matches the occlusion strategy used by several
prominent mitigation methods \cite{Burns_2018_WomenAlsoSnowboard,
	Wang_2019_BalancedDatasetsNotEnough}, the AUC remains at 70.8\% on COCO and 63.1\% on
OpenImages, directly undermining the core assumption of fairness-through-blindness
approaches. Class Activation Map analysis explains why: when the person is occluded, the
model shifts attention onto background objects, associating indoor objects with female
labels and outdoor objects with male labels.

\subsubsection*{Contextual Object Artifacts}
\label{subsubsec:meister_objects}

A logistic regression classifier trained on a binary object-presence vector achieves 75.4\%
AUC on COCO, with objects most predictive of female-labelled images including hair dryers,
handbags, and teddy bears, and those most predictive of male-labelled images including
skateboards, ties, and snowboards. Critically, gendered information is broadly distributed
across the object vocabulary rather than concentrated in a few obviously gendered objects:
30 randomly selected objects from the 80 annotated COCO categories still yield 66.8\% AUC,
confirming that no small subset of objects could be removed to meaningfully eliminate
object-level artifacts.

\subsubsection*{Analysis of Fairness-Through-Blindness Methods}
\label{subsubsec:meister_ftb}

Applying the adversarial debiasing approach of Wang et
al.~\cite{Wang_2019_BalancedDatasetsNotEnough} to COCO images reveals that 76.8\% of
removed pixels come from the background rather than the person, with outdoor scene objects
such as giraffes, trains, and buses most frequently affected. Because gendered signals are
spread throughout the image rather than localised to the person, the method suppresses
scene-level information necessary for downstream tasks rather than the person-centric
gendered information it was designed to target.

\subsubsection*{Discussion and Broader Implications}
\label{subsubsec:meister_discussion}

The authors draw three main conclusions. First, gender artifacts are so widely distributed
across image structure that removing them without also removing task-relevant information is
not feasible. Second, if a model can infer gender labels from mean pixel colour or the
presence of a skateboard, then what it has learned reflects the compositional biases of the
dataset rather than gender expression in any meaningful sense. Third, the authors recommend
moving away from fairness-through-blindness approaches towards fairness-aware methods that
explicitly account for distributional differences across demographic groups.

\section{Demographic Annotation in Large-Scale Image Datasets}
\label{sec:demographic_annotation}

Studying demographic bias in large-scale visual datasets requires annotating images with
sensitive attributes such as gender and skin tone. Unlike object category labels where a
clear ground truth can in principle be defined, demographic annotations are contested,
sitting at the intersection of biological, social, perceptual, and cultural factors. This
section surveys annotation practices, examines sources of measurement bias and label
uncertainty, reviews skin tone annotation frameworks, and outlines the core limitations of
visual demographic inference.

\subsection{Human and Automated Annotation Practices}
\label{subsec:annotation_practices}

Demographic attributes in visual datasets have traditionally been labelled by human
annotators. Early benchmarks such as Labelled Faces in the Wild \cite{Huang_2007_LFW}
exhibited variation in gender and race but were not designed for fairness analysis.
MS-COCO \cite{Lin_2014_COCO} includes the category person without structured demographic
annotations, while OpenImages \cite{Kuznetsova_2020_OpenImagesV4} adds subclasses such as
man and woman without a fairness-driven protocol. The More Inclusive Annotations for
People (MIAP) dataset \cite{Schumann_2021_StepInclusivePeopleAnnotations} introduces
non-binary gender presentation labels under a formal multi-annotator framework. These
efforts demonstrate that human demographic annotation is feasible at scale but is
methodologically complex, with disagreement and label uncertainty intrinsic to the task.

Inter-annotator agreement for demographic labels is typically lower than for object
categories, reflecting the interpretive ambiguity of socially constructed attributes.
Aroyo and Welty \cite{Aroyo_2015_CrowdTruth} argue that annotator disagreement often
encodes genuine differences in perception and interpretation, and that collapsing
annotations to a single majority label can obscure meaningful variation.

At the scale of web-scraped datasets, manual annotation is not practically feasible.
Large-scale studies therefore rely on automated demographic inference using pre-trained
classifiers. FairFace \cite{Karkkainen_2021_FairFace} is commonly used due to its improved
demographic coverage relative to earlier benchmarks.

A purpose-built benchmark for evaluating automated demographic classifiers is the Casual
Conversations v2 (CCv2) dataset \cite{Porgali_2023_CCv2}, comprising over 26,000 video
clips from more than 5,000 consenting participants recorded across diverse lighting and
environmental conditions. Demographic labels in CCv2 were provided directly by participants
through self-reporting, while trained annotators labelled apparent skin tone using both
the Fitzpatrick Skin Type and Monk Skin Tone scales. This dual-annotation design separates
self-identified from perceived attributes, providing a principled basis for evaluating
whether automated classifiers capture perceived appearance rather than identity.

A complementary benchmark is the Fairness in Computer Vision Evaluation (FACET) dataset
\cite{Gustafson_2023_FACET}, providing annotations for approximately 32,000 images drawn
from the Segment Anything 1 Billion (SA-1B) dataset, covering perceived gender
presentation, perceived skin tone using the Monk Skin Tone scale, perceived age group, and
additional fairness-relevant attributes. Annotations were produced by trained human raters
under a structured multi-stage pipeline and support systematic evaluation across demographic
attributes and their intersections.

However, automated annotation introduces a structural circularity. Demographic classifiers
are themselves trained on human-annotated datasets that encode the same perceptual
subjectivities and representational imbalances described above. As demonstrated by
Buolamwini and Gebru \cite{Buolamwini_2018_GenderShades}, performance disparities in
commercial gender classifiers reflect underlying dataset imbalances. When such models are
used to annotate new datasets, they propagate the statistical structure and potential biases
of their training data into downstream analyses, transforming annotation bias from an
observable human disagreement into a systematic model-mediated measurement artefact that is
no longer directly observable or correctable.

\subsection{Measurement Bias and Label Uncertainty}
\label{subsec:measurement_bias}

Measurement bias arises when the label assignment process systematically distorts the
metric it intends to measure \cite{Mehrabi_2022_SurveyBiasFairnessMachine}. Unlike object
categories, demographic attributes lack stable visual ground truth and are inferred from
perceptual cues, making measurement error often structured rather than random. Buolamwini
and Gebru \cite{Buolamwini_2018_GenderShades} show that darker-skinned females experience
significantly higher misclassification rates than lighter-skinned males. When automated
classifiers trained on such data annotate large-scale web datasets, these subgroup-specific
error patterns become embedded in the generated labels.

These issues are particularly consequential when demographic labels function as covariates
against which feature distributions are measured. Structured annotation error can produce
apparent correlations between visual artefacts and demographic attributes that reflect
classifier error patterns rather than properties of the underlying data. Without ground
truth labels at scale, disentangling genuine distributional structure from measurement
artefact becomes statistically intractable. Careful selection and documentation of
annotation models is therefore essential when demographic attributes are used to audit
spurious correlations in large-scale visual datasets.

\subsection{Skin Tone Annotation Frameworks}
\label{subsec:skin_tone_frameworks}

The most widely used skin tone framework in computer vision is the Fitzpatrick Skin Type
(FST) scale, originally developed in dermatology to classify sun-reactive skin response to
ultraviolet exposure \cite{Fitzpatrick_1988_SkinTypes}. Its categories are anchored in
self-reported photoreactivity rather than directly observable colour properties, creating a
construct mismatch when applied to image-based annotation. Subsequent work has documented
the compression of variation at the darker end of the scale \cite{Groh_2021_Fitzpatrick17k},
reducing sensitivity precisely where subgroup disparities are often most pronounced.

The Monk Skin Tone (MST) scale was developed to address these limitations as a ten-point
ordinal framework explicitly designed for technology and machine learning applications
\cite{Schumann_2024_MonkSkinTone}. Unlike Fitzpatrick, the MST scale is anchored to visual
reference swatches intended for photographic assessment rather than sun-reactive history.
A large-scale survey comparing Fitzpatrick, MST, and a 40-shade commercial palette found
that Fitzpatrick was perceived as significantly less representative, particularly among
darker-skinned participants, while the ten-point MST scale was perceived as comparably
inclusive to the much finer-grained commercial palette \cite{Heldreth_2024_WhichSkinToneMeasures}.

Empirical work evaluating MST estimation under diverse lighting demonstrates substantial
degradation in fine-grained ten-class performance, particularly in intermediate tone ranges
\cite{Mbatha_2024_SkinToneLighting}. When tones are grouped into broader light, medium,
and dark categories, estimation stability improves markedly across lighting conditions.
For large-scale web-scraped imagery where illumination is uncontrolled, grouped treatment
provides greater robustness and reduces boundary-level annotation noise. Grouping in this
context is not a simplification of the construct but a strategy to reduce measurement
variance that could otherwise obscure or artificially induce spurious correlations.

A further methodological risk is shortcut learning during skin tone estimation. Experimental
evidence shows that full-image CNN models exploit background, clothing, or contextual
artefacts correlated with skin tone labels rather than pigmentation itself, as revealed by
Grad-CAM analyses, whereas models trained on segmented facial skin regions exhibit
activations concentrated on skin pixels \cite{Matias_2024_EnhancingFairnessMST}. For this
reason, skin tone classification in this thesis is applied to detected facial skin regions
rather than full images.

Schumann et al.~\cite{Schumann_2024_MonkSkinTone} introduced the Monk Skin Tone Examples
(MST-E) dataset comprising 1,515 images and 31 videos of 19 subjects spanning the full
ten-point MST scale, photographed across a range of poses, expressions, and lighting
conditions. Final annotations were provided by the creator of the MST scale, Dr Ellis
Monk. The dataset was designed to support annotator training and to investigate annotation
consistency under varying environmental conditions, and also enables evaluation of automated
skin tone estimation methods across specific capture conditions rather than relying solely
on aggregate accuracy metrics.

\subsection{Limitations of Visual Demographic Inference}
\label{subsec:demographic_limitations}

A fundamental limitation of demographic annotation from visual data is the distinction
between perceived attributes and self-identified attributes. Systems that infer gender from
images predict whether a subject's appearance conforms to culturally dominant norms of
gendered presentation, not gender identity. Scheuerman et
al.~\cite{Scheuerman_2019_HowComputersSeeGender} demonstrate that commercial gender
classification systems operationalise gender as a binary perceptual category and exhibit
systematic failures on individuals whose presentation diverges from normative expectations.
Consequently, when generative outputs are compared to demographic statistics such as labour
force participation rates, the annotation variable and the statistical benchmark may refer
to different constructs.

A second limitation concerns the instability of photometric inference under varying
acquisition conditions. Skin tone classifiers are sensitive to illumination colour
temperature, exposure, and post-processing. In uncontrolled web-scale datasets such as
Re-LAION-5B, images are captured under heterogeneous conditions, creating a confound
between acquisition artefacts and demographic inference quality. This issue is further
compounded in synthetic imagery, where lighting is a learned prior embedded in the
generative model rather than an external environmental factor.

A related conceptual distinction must be drawn between race and skin tone. Race is a
socially constructed category shaped by historical, political, and cultural processes that
cannot be reliably inferred from visual appearance alone. Skin tone is a photometric
property of visible surface pigmentation. While correlated with racial categorisation in
many societies, the two constructs are not equivalent, and fairness audits that
operationalise race through perceived visual categorisation introduce additional layers of
annotator subjectivity \cite{Raji_2019_ActionableAuditing}. Skin tone, though still
perceptually mediated, is more directly grounded in observable colour properties and more
closely aligned with the low-level image features examined in spurious feature analysis.

Taken together, these limitations highlight that demographic labels derived from visual
inference function as measurement instruments rather than ground truth variables. Recognising
this constraint is essential when interpreting spurious feature distributions in both
web-scraped datasets and generative model outputs.

\section{Inference-Time Debiasing of Text-to-Image Diffusion Models}
\label{sec:inference_debiasing}

Approaches to mitigating demographic bias in text-to-image diffusion models can be
categorised by the stage of the pipeline at which they intervene. Training-time methods
address bias through modifications to the training data or objective function
\cite{Yang_2020_FairerDatasets, Zhang_2018_MitigatingUnwantedBiasesAdversarial}.
Fine-tuning methods adjust pre-trained weights on demographic reference corpora
\cite{Friedrich_2023_FairDiffusionInstructingTextToImage}. Both families require
substantial computational resources and high-quality annotated data, placing them beyond
reach for practitioners working with pre-trained deployed systems \cite{Jiang_2025_FairGen}.

Inference-time methods operate on a frozen model during generation, requiring no retraining
and no access to weights beyond those publicly available. This family is particularly
relevant for widely deployed open-source systems such as Stable Diffusion v1.5, where the
only available intervention points are the conditioning signal, the sampling trajectory,
and the model's internal attention mechanisms. The methods surveyed below address
demographic bias through three distinct mechanisms: editing internal model representations
via closed-form weight modifications, manipulating the text conditioning signal through
learned inclusive embeddings, and guiding the denoising trajectory through external signals
targeting spatial structure or colour distribution.

\subsubsection*{Weight Editing: Unified Concept Editing}
\label{subsubsec:uce}

Unified Concept Editing (UCE) \cite{Gandikota_2024_UCE} directly edits the cross-attention
projections of a diffusion model through a closed-form solution, without gradient-based
training. The value projection matrices $W_v$ of the U-Net's cross-attention layers encode
associations between text token embeddings and the visual features attended to during
denoising. UCE frames debiasing as a constrained least-squares problem over $W_v$ at each
attention layer: given target concept embeddings representing biased associations to be
corrected and anchor embeddings representing unrelated content to be preserved, UCE computes
a closed-form update $\Delta W_v$ that minimises change to anchor representations while
steering target representations toward a more balanced demographic prior. This closed-form
solution is derived from a linearisation of the attention layer's behaviour around the
target token embeddings, requiring no forward passes beyond computing a small number of
text embeddings. Because the edit is applied layer-wise and accumulated across attention
layers, each layer contributes independently to the overall concept redirection, and the
closed-form solution at each layer does not account for the downstream effects of edits
applied to other layers.

A key strength is scalability to concurrent edits across multiple concepts, demonstrated
across up to 35 occupational prompts alongside style erasure and content moderation within
a single edited model. However, because the edit applies uniformly across all spatial
positions and all prompts that activate the targeted concept representation, UCE cannot
selectively debias outputs in specific image regions or under specific contextual
conditions. The linear approximation underlying the closed-form solution also constrains
the method's ability to correct strongly non-linear biases embedded simultaneously across
multiple layers. It also provides no mechanism for specifying a precise target demographic
distribution.

\subsubsection*{Inclusive Prompt Embeddings: ITI-GEN}
\label{subsubsec:iti_gen}

ITI-GEN \cite{Zhang_2023_ITIGEN} operates at the level of the text conditioning signal
rather than the model weights. It begins from the observation that demographic attributes
such as skin tone are difficult to express unambiguously through natural language, and
resolves this by replacing linguistic specification with image-grounded specification.
The method learns a set of inclusive tokens, which are short learnable embedding vectors
inserted into the CLIP text encoder's input space, optimised to produce generated images
uniformly distributed across the categories of a target demographic attribute.

For each attribute category, a set of reference images is selected, and the corresponding
token embedding is trained via two losses through backpropagation through the frozen
diffusion model. A direction alignment loss encourages the learned token to move the text
embedding toward the CLIP image embedding of the reference images for that category. A
semantic consistency loss penalises divergence from the original prompt's semantic content,
ensuring the inclusive token supplements rather than overwrites the user's intended
generation context. For $A$ attributes each with $K$ categories, one requires $A \times K$
reference image sets rather than $K^A$ joint combinations, and the resulting tokens are
composable at inference time by concatenation. ITI-GEN does not modify model weights and
is directly compatible with ControlNet \cite{LZhang_2023_ControlNet} and other conditioning
frameworks.

A reproducibility study by Gallo Fernandez et al.~\cite{GalloFernandez_2024_ITIGENRepro}
identifies two important limitations. First, ITI-GEN is susceptible to learning spurious
proxy features from its reference images: if reference images for a demographic category
are systematically correlated with background, clothing, or lighting, the learned token may
encode these spurious cues rather than the target attribute itself. Second, the exponential
growth of the joint attribute combination space with increasing numbers of attributes makes
inclusive generation for all joint combinations increasingly difficult.

\subsubsection*{Self-Discovering Latent Directions: FairGen}
\label{subsubsec:fairgen}

FairGen \cite{Jiang_2025_FairGen} addresses a structural limitation shared by both UCE and
ITI-GEN: their dependence on externally curated reference datasets or annotation models to
define target demographic directions. FairGen instead discovers demographic latent
directions directly from the model through a self-supervised procedure, attaching
lightweight adapters to the cross-attention layers of the frozen U-Net and optimising them
to learn the direction in the model's latent feature space along which a target attribute
varies. This is achieved by manipulating the noise prediction function through a noise
composition objective that exploits the structure of the DDPM training process, allowing
the latent direction for a given demographic attribute to be extracted without requiring
any labelled demographic data.

Orthogonal regularisation between adapters for different attributes is a critical design
choice: without it, adapters trained on correlated attributes such as gender and age, which
co-vary in training data, tend to encode overlapping directions that interfere
destructively when activated together, degrading both fairness and image quality. At
inference time, a distribution indicator tracks the demographic composition of images
generated so far in a batch and steers subsequent generation to correct any accumulated
imbalance, enforcing fairness globally across a batch rather than independently per image.
The primary limitation is that the discovered directions are model-specific and
non-transferable, requiring the self-discovering training process to be repeated for each
base model architecture.

\subsubsection*{Spatial Conditioning: ControlNet}
\label{subsubsec:controlnet}

ControlNet \cite{LZhang_2023_ControlNet} adds fine-grained spatial conditioning to a
pre-trained diffusion model, enabling the structure of generated images to be constrained
by auxiliary signals such as pose skeletons, depth maps, Canny edge maps, or semantic
segmentation masks. The central architectural challenge is to introduce spatial
conditioning without destabilising the large pre-trained backbone. ControlNet resolves this
through a trainable copy of the frozen U-Net encoder and middle block that receives the
additional conditioning signal and processes it through the same encoder structure. The
outputs are injected back into the frozen backbone via residual connections at each encoder
level, allowing the decoder to integrate spatial information during the upsampling pass
where image structure is synthesised.

To prevent the randomly initialised conditioning branch from corrupting the frozen
backbone during early training, all residual connections are implemented as zero-initialised
convolution layers. At training onset these produce zero output, so the backbone behaves
identically to the unmodified model. As training proceeds, the zero convolution weights
grow from zero in proportion to the gradient signal, introducing conditioning information
progressively. Multiple ControlNet signals can be composed at inference time by summing
their residual contributions, enabling simultaneous constraint of multiple spatial
properties without retraining.

In the context of debiasing, ControlNet is relevant because many demographic spurious
features operate at the structural level. As documented by Meister et
al.~\cite{Meister_2023_GenderArtifactsinVisualDatasets}, body pose distributions,
person-to-frame scale, and spatial framing patterns constitute significant gender artifacts
not removed by purely semantic or colouristic debiasing interventions. By providing an
explicit structural conditioning signal such as a neutral canonical pose skeleton,
ControlNet can constrain the spatial properties of generated images independently of the
semantic and demographic properties governed by the text prompt and inclusive token
embeddings, enabling both forms of control to be composed without mutual interference.

\subsubsection*{Colour Distribution Guidance: SW-Guidance and Chamfer-Based Alignment}
\label{subsubsec:colour_guidance}

A further class of inference-time methods targets the colour statistics of generated
images directly, motivated by the observation that text-to-image models trained on
aesthetically filtered web-scraped corpora learn strong colour priors that may correlate
with demographic attributes. Consistent with the spurious feature analysis of Meister et
al.~\cite{Meister_2023_GenderArtifactsinVisualDatasets}, who demonstrate that mean RGB
channel values are sufficient to discriminate gender-labelled images above chance, colour
distributions in training data can function as low-level proxies for demographic group
membership.

SW-Guidance \cite{Lobashev_2025_SWGuidance} is a training-free method that introduces
colour conditioning into the diffusion sampling process by modifying the noise prediction
at each denoising step. At each step the gradient of the Sliced 1-Wasserstein distance
between the colour distribution of the current partially denoised image and a reference
palette is computed and added to the denoising network's noise prediction, functioning as
an additional guidance term analogous to CFG \cite{Ho_2022_CFG}. A notable practical
limitation is that computing the guidance gradient requires backpropagation through the
entire denoising U-Net at every timestep, introducing substantial overhead that makes
SW-Guidance impractical for large-scale image generation.

Shum et al.~\cite{Shum_2025_ColorAlignment} propose an alternative colour alignment
approach that avoids backpropagation through the denoising network. Their method projects
diffusion terms, either the partially denoised latent representation or the predicted clean
image $\hat{x}_0$, directly into a conditional colour manifold defined by a reference
palette, using the Chamfer distance to establish correspondences between the colour point
clouds of the generated and reference images:

\begin{equation}
	d_\text{Chamfer}(X, Y) = \sum_{x \in X} \min_{y \in Y}
	\|x - y\|_2^2 + \sum_{y \in Y} \min_{x \in X} \|x - y\|_2^2
\end{equation}

At each denoising step where colour alignment is active, an iterative matching procedure
computes the Chamfer-optimal correspondence and takes a gradient step to project the
predicted $\hat{x}_0$ toward the reference distribution, which is then used in place of
the original predicted clean image in the standard DDPM recomposition formula. Alignment
is applied only during early denoising timesteps, reflecting the empirical observation that
global colour structure is established early in the denoising trajectory while fine
structural and semantic details emerge during later steps. Below a configurable threshold,
alignment is disabled to avoid corrupting the fine-grained content forming during late
denoising.

\section{Occupations as a Probe for Demographic Bias in Text-to-Image Generation}
\label{sec:occupations}

Having established how bias manifests in datasets and propagates through generative models,
an appropriate probing framework is required to measure these effects empirically. Occupations
represent one of the most well-validated analytical lenses for this purpose. Job titles carry historically accumulated societal
associations between professional roles and demographic attributes, extensively documented
in real-world labour statistics and deeply reflected in the large-scale text and image
corpora on which modern models are trained. Neutral occupation prompts therefore provide a
transparent and interpretable window onto a model's learned demographic priors, making them
a standard experimental instrument across both natural language processing and text-to-image
generation research.

\subsection{Occupational Stereotyping in Language and Vision Systems}
\label{subsubsec:occupational_stereo}

Evidence that AI systems acquire and reproduce occupational stereotypes is well established
across a range of model families. Nadeem et al.~\cite{Nadeem_2020_StereoSet} demonstrate
through their large-scale StereoSet benchmark, covering 120 profession-related target terms,
that all major pretrained language models including BERT, GPT-2, RoBERTa, and XLNet exhibit
significant stereotypical preferences in profession-related contexts. Notably, they find a
consistent positive relationship between a model's language modelling ability and the
strength of its stereotypical biases, suggesting that stereotype acquisition is a systematic
consequence of learning from real-world text distributions. This is directly relevant to
the present work because the CLIP text encoders that condition text-to-image models are
pretrained on precisely such distributions, providing a theoretically grounded pathway
through which occupational stereotypes propagate into generated imagery.

In the vision-language domain, Harrison et al.~\cite{Harrison_2023_RunLikeAGirl}
demonstrate that human annotators systematically apply role-based names more frequently to
male athletes than to female athletes performing identical activities, with an average of
46\% sport-related names for male subjects versus 35\% for female subjects. Because
captioning datasets of this kind form part of the training corpora for large
vision-language models, such annotation asymmetries constitute a direct mechanism through
which occupational concepts become demographically skewed in model representations.

\subsection{Occupational Bias in Text-to-Image Models}
\label{subsubsec:naik_occupations}

Within text-to-image generation, Naik and Nushi~\cite{Naik_2023_SocialBiasesThroughTheTextToImageGenerationLens}
provide a comprehensive audit confirming that occupation prompts reliably surface severe
representational biases in both DALL-E v2 and Stable Diffusion v1. Generating images with
neutral prompts such as a photo of a doctor and annotating outputs for perceived gender,
race, and age, they find that model outputs deviate dramatically from real-world
occupational demographics as reported by the US Bureau of Labor Statistics. Occupations
such as chief executive officer and computer programmer yield images that are almost
exclusively male and white, while nurse and housekeeper yield almost exclusively female
images. Chuang et al.~\cite{Chuang_2023_DebiasingVisionLanguageModelsViaBiasedPrompts}
further demonstrate that these biases are embedded in the structure of the CLIP text
embedding space itself, with demographic directions geometrically separable from
occupational content, confirming that occupation prompts are a particularly reliable and
interpretable probe for the demographic biases arising from web-scale training data.

\subsection{Occupation Classification: The ISCO-08 Framework}
\label{subsubsec:isco}

For occupation-based probing to yield generalisable results, the prompt set must be drawn
from a principled taxonomy covering the full breadth of occupational space rather than
over-representing stereotypically gendered or racialised roles selected by convenience.
The International Labour Organization's International Standard Classification of Occupations
(ISCO-08) \cite{ILO_ISCO08} provides this foundation, organising occupations into a
four-level hierarchy: ten major groups, 43 sub-major groups, 130 minor groups, and 436
unit groups, each assigned a numeric code and skill-level designation ranging from
elementary occupations at level 1 through highly qualified professionals at level 4.

In this thesis, ISCO-08 serves as the organising framework for the occupation prompt set.
By sampling job titles systematically across all ten major groups, the resulting set spans
a representative range of social prestige, skill requirements, and cultural connotation,
ensuring that bias measurements reflect the model's general behaviour across occupational
space rather than being driven by a small subset of roles with strong stereotypical
associations. The use of an internationally standardised classification also facilitates
comparison with prior work that has likewise adopted ISCO-08 as a reference
point \cite{Naik_2023_SocialBiasesThroughTheTextToImageGenerationLens}.
