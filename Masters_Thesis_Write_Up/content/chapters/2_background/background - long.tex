\section{Re-LAION-5B Dataset}
Re-LAION-5B is a large-scale, openly released image-text dataset comprising approximately 5.85 billion image-text pairs harvested from the publicly accessible portions of the internet via Common Crawl, a non-profit organisation that continuously indexes web content and makes its archived crawl data freely available to researchers \cite{Schuhmann_2022_Laion5b, CommonCrawl_2007_CommonCrawl}. The dataset is a re-release of the original LAION-5B, itself introduced by Schuhmann et al. \cite{Schuhmann_2022_Laion5b} as one of the largest publicly available multimodal training corpora at the time of its release. The re-release was motivated by the discovery of \acrshort{csam} within the original corpus, with Re-LAION-5B representing a re-filtered version produced through improved safety filtering pipelines, including enhanced perceptual hashing and classifier-based detection methods designed to remove harmful content \cite{Laion_2024_Relaion5B}.

The construction pipeline of the original LAION-5B, which Re-LAION-5B is derived from, is structured as follows. HTML pages indexed by Common Crawl are parsed to extract image URLs paired with their associated \texttt{alt} text, yielding raw image-caption pairs at web scale. These pairs are subsequently filtered using a CLIP-based semantic similarity score \cite{Radford_2021_CLIP}, computed as the cosine similarity between image and text embeddings using OpenAI's ViT-B/32 CLIP model for English pairs and a multilingual CLIP variant for all others. As described by Schuhmann et al. \cite{Schuhmann_2022_Laion5b}, pairs below a cosine similarity of 0.28 were discarded for English content and below 0.26 for non-English content, a step that removed approximately 90\% of the original 50 billion candidate pairs. Additional filtering stages remove near-duplicate pairs using locality-sensitive hashing, apply NSFW classifiers to exclude explicit content, and enforce minimum resolution and caption length requirements to discard low-quality samples.

The resulting dataset is stratified by detected language into three subsets: LAION-2B-en, comprising approximately 2.32 billion English-language image-text pairs; LAION-2B-multi, comprising approximately 2.26 billion pairs spanning over 100 other languages; and LAION-1B-nolang, comprising approximately 1.27 billion samples for which no language could be reliably detected, which frequently correspond to images of products, places, or content dominated by proper nouns \cite{Schuhmann_2022_Laion5b}. Together these three subsets constitute the full LAION-5B corpus of approximately 5.85 billion pairs.

A further aesthetics-filtered derivative was constructed exclusively from LAION-2B-en. Using a linear classifier trained on CLIP ViT-L/14 image embeddings to predict human aesthetic ratings, Schuhmann \cite{Schuhmann_2022_LaionAesthetics} produced a series of nested subsets of LAION-2B-en filtered by predicted aesthetic score. The subset used for training Stable Diffusion v1.5, referred to as LAION-Aesthetics v2 5+, retains only those pairs with a predicted aesthetic score exceeding 5.0 out of 10, an original image resolution of at least 512$\times$512 pixels, and an estimated watermark probability below 0.5 \cite{StableDiffusion_2022_SD15modelcard}, yielding approximately 600 million image-text pairs. LAION-Aesthetics is therefore not a separate corpus but a quality-filtered offshoot of the English-language component of LAION-5B.


Despite the scale and ambition of the dataset, several significant concerns have been raised regarding its content. Birhane et al. \cite{Birhane_2021_MultiModalDatasetsMisogynyPornography} conducted an audit of LAION-400M, a predecessor to LAION-5B, and identified substantial quantities of misogynistic, pornographic, and racially derogatory content embedded within the corpus, reflecting the unfiltered nature of web-scraped data. These findings were later extended to LAION-5B itself, reinforcing concerns that automated safety filtering is insufficient to remove harmful material at billion-scale. Beyond explicit harmful content, the web-scraped construction process introduces systematic demographic and representational imbalances, as the statistical composition of online imagery reflects existing societal inequities in content production, platform access, and visual representation \cite{Birhane_2021_MultiModalDatasetsMisogynyPornography, Mehrabi_2022_SurveyBiasFairnessMachine}.

A practically significant limitation of the Re-LAION-5B release concerns the availability of pre-computed embeddings. The original LAION-5B release included pre-computed CLIP image and text embeddings alongside image URLs and captions, enabling efficient nearest-neighbour retrieval and semantic search without requiring users to independently process raw images. These embeddings underpinned tools such as the LAION CLIP retrieval service, which allowed researchers to query the dataset semantically. In the Re-LAION-5B re-release, these pre-computed embeddings were not made publicly available, as the re-filtering process altered the dataset composition and the associated embedding infrastructure was not reconstructed at the time of release \cite{Laion_2024_Relaion5B}. As a result, any research requiring embedding-based retrieval from Re-LAION-5B must independently compute CLIP embeddings from raw image downloads, introducing substantial computational overhead and making large-scale semantic queries considerably more difficult to execute than was the case with the original release. For this thesis, this absence necessitates the derivation of image embeddings as part of the data acquisition pipeline.

Furthermore a practical consequence of Re-LAION-5B's web-sourced construction
is link attrition: the proportion of URLs that resolve successfully
degrades over time as hosting services expire, content is removed, or
domains change structure. This phenomenon, commonly termed link rot or
URL decay, is well-documented in the web archiving
literature~\cite{Klein_2014_LinkRot} and is an inherent property of
any dataset whose images are stored as references to live web resources
rather than as archived binaries. Schuhmann et al.~\cite{Schuhmann_2022_Laion5b}
acknowledge that a fraction of LAION-5B URLs will be unreachable at any
given point following release, and this fraction grows with elapsed time.
The effective resolution rate depends critically on how much time has
elapsed since the crawl from which the URLs were collected. For
Re-LAION-5B, which re-filters URLs originally crawled by Common
Crawl~\cite{CommonCrawl_2007_CommonCrawl}, the gap between crawl date and download
date introduces a decay component that cannot be precisely quantified
without access to per-URL crawl timestamps. This has direct
methodological implications for any work that downloads images from the
dataset after its release, as the surviving image pool is not a random
sample of the original corpus but a temporally filtered subset shaped by
domain-specific hosting practices and content removal patterns.

Beyond link attrition, web-scraped datasets exhibit systematic domain
concentration: a disproportionate share of images originates from a small
number of large hosting platforms rather than being drawn uniformly from
the breadth of the web. This is a structural consequence of web crawling
methodology, which can result in disproportionate representation of content
from highly interconnected or high-visibility domains relative to smaller
independent sites. Birhane et al.~\cite{Birhane_2021_MultiModalDatasetsMisogynyPornography}
demonstrate this in their audit of LAION-400M, showing that content
characteristics --- including the prevalence of hypersexualised imagery and
stereotypical material --- reflect the typology of source platforms, and
that structural biases introduced at the crawling stage propagate directly
into the resulting dataset. Zeng et al.~\cite{Zeng_2024_UnderstandingBiasinLargeScaleVisualDatasets}
similarly demonstrate that large-scale web-scraped datasets are reliably
distinguishable by neural networks based on their semantic and structural
content, with dataset-specific object distributions and scene themes
consistent with originating from distinct web sub-ecosystems. The practical
consequence of domain concentration for bias analysis is that observed
feature distributions in the retrieved subset may partially reflect the
visual conventions of a small number of dominant source domains rather than
the broader distributional properties of the dataset as a whole.

\section{Stable Diffusion: Architecture, Training, and Dataset Lineage}

Generative models encompass a variety of approaches, including Generative 
Adversarial Networks (GANs) \cite{Goodfellow_2014_GAN}, Variational 
Autoencoders (VAEs) \cite{Kingma_2013_VAE}, and diffusion models. Diffusion 
models offer several advantages over their counterparts: unlike GANs, they 
excel in training stability and diverse image generation, avoiding the 
mode collapse and training instability that frequently afflict adversarial 
approaches \cite{Dhariwal_2021_DiffusionBeatGANs}. They also bypass the 
surrogate loss limitation inherent in VAEs, which constrains the fidelity 
of reconstructed outputs. These properties have made diffusion models the 
dominant architecture for high-quality image synthesis.

Stable Diffusion is a family of open-source text-to-image generative models 
based on the Latent Diffusion Model (LDM) framework introduced by Rombach 
et al.\ \cite{Rombach_2022_CVPR}. The central architectural innovation of 
LDMs is the decoupling of the generative process from the pixel space of 
the original image. A Variational Autoencoder \cite{Kingma_2013_VAE} is 
first trained to learn a compressed latent representation of images, with 
its encoder mapping high-resolution inputs into a lower-dimensional latent 
space and its decoder reconstructing images from latent codes. Once trained, 
the VAE encoder and decoder are frozen, and all subsequent generative 
modelling is performed within this learned latent space rather than at full 
pixel resolution. This compression dramatically reduces the computational 
cost of training and inference relative to pixel-space diffusion models 
such as DALL-E \cite{Ramesh_2021_DALLE} or earlier score-based generative 
models \cite{Song_2020_ScoreMatching}, making high-resolution image 
synthesis tractable at scale.

The diffusion process itself follows the Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic 
Model (DDPM) framework \cite{Ho_2020_DDPM}, in which a forward process 
incrementally corrupts a clean latent representation by adding Gaussian 
noise over a fixed number of timesteps $T$, such that the latent 
progressively loses structure until it approximates pure Gaussian noise. 
A learned reverse process --- implemented as a U-Net 
\cite{Ronneberger_2015_UNet} backbone --- then iteratively denoises a 
noisy latent to recover a clean sample. At inference time, generation 
begins from a sample of pure Gaussian noise and proceeds through repeated 
application of the denoising network over multiple timesteps, producing a 
coherent latent which is then decoded into a full image by the VAE decoder.

Conditioning on text prompts is achieved through cross-attention mechanisms 
integrated into the intermediate layers of the U-Net backbone. Text inputs 
are encoded using a pretrained CLIP text encoder \cite{Radford_2021_CLIP}, 
producing a sequence of text embeddings that are injected into the 
denoising network via cross-attention at multiple spatial resolutions, 
enabling the model to align generated image content with the semantic 
content of the input prompt.

\subsection*{Classifier-Free Guidance}\label{subsec:cfg}

During inference, Classifier-Free Guidance (CFG) \cite{Ho_2022_CFG} is 
applied to strengthen the influence of the text conditioning signal on 
generated outputs. CFG works by jointly training a single model to 
function both as a conditional model --- predicting noise given a text 
embedding --- and as an unconditional model, where the text conditioning 
is randomly replaced with a null token during training. In the case of 
Stable Diffusion v1.5 specifically, this was implemented by dropping the 
text conditioning signal entirely with a probability of 10\% during 
training \cite{StableDiffusion_2022_SD15modelcard}, which is what makes CFG sampling possible at inference time. At inference, the model performs two forward passes 
per denoising step: one conditioned on the text prompt and one 
unconditioned. The final noise estimate is then computed as:

\begin{equation}
	\tilde{\epsilon}_\theta(z_t, c) = \epsilon_\theta(z_t, \emptyset) 
	+ s \cdot \bigl(\epsilon_\theta(z_t, c) - 
	\epsilon_\theta(z_t, \emptyset)\bigr)
\end{equation}

where $z_t$ is the noisy latent at timestep $t$, $c$ is the text 
conditioning, $\emptyset$ denotes the null unconditional token, and 
$s \geq 1$ is the guidance scale. The guidance scale $s$ controls 
the degree to which the model is steered toward the conditioning 
signal: higher values produce outputs more strongly adherent to the 
prompt, while lower values allow more stochastic variation. However, 
this strengthening of the conditioning signal has a direct implication 
for bias: at higher guidance scales, the model is pushed more strongly 
toward its learned prior over the training distribution. Any demographic 
stereotypes or spurious correlations encoded during training --- such as 
the association between certain occupational prompts and particular 
skin-tone or gender distributions --- are therefore amplified more 
aggressively at higher CFG scales, making guidance scale a relevant 
parameter in the analysis of generative bias.

\subsection*{Training Lineage}

Stable Diffusion v1.5, the model examined in this thesis, was released 
by RunwayML and constitutes a continuation of training from Stable 
Diffusion v1.2, itself initialised from v1.1 weights produced by CompVis 
\cite{StableDiffusion_2022_SD15modelcard}. Training was conducted on the LAION-Aesthetics v2 
5+ subset \cite{Schuhmann_2022_LaionAesthetics}, comprising approximately 600 million 
image-text pairs filtered from LAION-2B-en on the basis of a predicted 
aesthetic quality score exceeding 5.0, an original resolution of at 
least 512$\times$512 pixels, and an estimated watermark probability 
below 0.5. The model was trained at 512$\times$512 pixel resolution 
using the AdamW optimiser with a batch size of 2048, and the 10\% 
text-conditioning dropout described above was applied throughout 
\cite{StableDiffusion_2022_SD15modelcard}. Critically, the training corpus is a direct 
derivative of the broader LAION-2B-en dataset, inheriting its 
web-scraped composition, demographic and representational imbalances, 
and the spurious statistical regularities documented in Section 2.1. 
The open-source availability of both model weights and the documented 
training data lineage makes Stable Diffusion v1.5 particularly 
well-suited for mechanistic bias analysis, as findings can be 
independently verified and directly linked to known properties of 
the training corpus.

Subsequent iterations in the Stable Diffusion family have progressively 
diverged from this openly documented training data lineage. Stable 
Diffusion 2.0 and 2.1, developed by Stability AI, were trained on a 
subset of LAION-5B filtered using a more stringent NSFW classifier and 
employed a larger OpenCLIP text encoder \cite{Cherti_2023_OpenCLIP} 
in place of the original CLIP encoder, but retained the LAION-scraped 
foundation. Stable Diffusion XL (SDXL) adopted an enlarged dual-encoder 
architecture and a multi-aspect ratio training procedure, but was trained 
on a proprietary dataset curated by Stability AI whose composition has 
not been publicly disclosed \cite{Podell_2023_SDXL}. More recent releases 
including Stable Diffusion 3 and Stable Diffusion 3.5 introduce rectified 
flow transformers as the denoising backbone and similarly rely on 
undisclosed proprietary training corpora \cite{Esser_2024_SD3}. This 
progressive shift away from openly documented training data reduces 
external reproducibility and substantially limits the capacity for 
independent bias auditing in later models. The absence of training data 
transparency in newer systems reinforces the methodological value of 
focusing the present analysis on Stable Diffusion v1.5, where the 
training data lineage remains traceable and findings can be grounded 
in documented dataset properties.

\subsection{Low-Rank Adaptation of Diffusion Models}

Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) is a parameter-efficient fine-tuning technique
introduced by Hu et al.~\cite{Hu_2021_LoRA} for adapting large pretrained
models without updating their full weight matrices. The central insight is
that the update $\Delta W$ applied to a weight matrix $W \in \mathbb{R}^{d
	\times k}$ during adaptation can be decomposed into the product of two
low-rank matrices $A \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times r}$ and $B \in \mathbb{R}^{r
	\times k}$, where the rank $r \ll \min(d, k)$:

\[
W' = W + \Delta W = W + AB
\]

Because only $A$ and $B$ are trained while $W$ is frozen, the number of
trainable parameters is reduced from $d \times k$ to $r(d + k)$, enabling
adaptation at a fraction of the computational cost of full fine-tuning.
LoRA adapters are applied to the attention projection matrices of the model
and can be fused directly into the base weights at inference time by
computing $W + AB$, eliminating any runtime overhead. In diffusion model
contexts, LoRA was adopted as the standard mechanism for lightweight
concept adaptation and style transfer~\cite{Hu_2021_LoRA}, and has
subsequently been extended to control a wide range of generative
behaviours.

Latent Consistency Models (LCMs) introduced a distillation-based training
objective that enables high-quality image generation in as few as two to
four denoising steps, compared to the twenty to fifty steps required by
standard DDPM samplers~\cite{Song_2023_LCM}. LCM-LoRA~\cite{Luo_2023_LCMLoRA}
applies the LCM distillation procedure as a LoRA adapter rather than
requiring full retraining of the base model. The adapter modifies the
denoising network's behaviour to implement a consistency function, ensuring
that predictions at different noise levels map to the same clean image,
thereby allowing the sampling trajectory to skip intermediate timesteps
without incurring the visual distortion associated with simply reducing the
step count on a standard DDPM model.

A critical property of speed-based LoRAs such as LCM-LoRA is that their
training objective is purely geometric: the adapter is optimised to
reproduce the output distribution of the base model more efficiently,
without any training signal derived from demographic content, aesthetic
labels, or semantic categories. The LCM distillation loss operates on the
consistency of latent-space trajectories across noise
levels~\cite{Song_2023_LCM}, and the adapter weights therefore encode
information about the structure of the denoising process rather than the
content of the training images. This distinguishes speed-based LoRAs
categorically from concept LoRAs~\cite{Hu_2021_LoRA} or style
LoRAs~\cite{Ruiz_2023_DreamBooth}, which are trained to shift the
model's semantic prior toward a specific concept or appearance. As a
consequence, applying LCM-LoRA to Stable Diffusion v1.5 preserves the
base model's learned demographic distribution intact: any representational
biases present in the frozen base model are reproduced by the accelerated
pipeline at precisely the same rate as they would be under standard DDPM
sampling. This property is directly relevant to the present work, where
the objective is to measure the demographic prior of the base model; use of
LCM-LoRA ensures that the acceleration mechanism is not a confounding
variable in the analysis.

\subsection{Aesthetic Quality Assessment in Large-Scale Image Datasets}
\label{subsec:aesthetic_scoring}

The aesthetic quality of images in web-scraped datasets varies enormously,
from professional photography to low-resolution thumbnails, compressed
product images, and partially rendered or corrupted files. To address
this, Schuhmann~\cite{Schuhmann_2022_LaionAesthetics} trained a linear
aesthetic predictor on top of CLIP ViT-L/14 image embeddings using human
preference ratings drawn from the Simulacra Aesthetic Captions (SAC)
dataset~\cite{Pressman_2022_SAC}, a collection of approximately 238,000 images
annotated by crowd workers for perceived visual quality on a scale from 1
to 10. The resulting predictor is a lightweight linear regression head that
maps a 768-dimensional CLIP embedding to a scalar aesthetic score,
inheriting CLIP's broad visual representations while requiring only a small
annotated training set. This predictor was used to construct the
LAION-Aesthetics v2 subsets, with the 5+ threshold retaining only those
images predicted to have an aesthetic score exceeding 5.0 out of 10. The
training corpus for Stable Diffusion v1.5, LAION-Aesthetics v2 5+,
therefore consists entirely of images that clear this threshold, comprising
approximately 600 million image-text pairs~\cite{StableDiffusion_2022_SD15modelcard}.

In the context of evaluating generative model outputs rather than curating
training data, aesthetic scoring serves a different purpose. Diffusion
models operating at reduced step counts can produce structurally coherent
images that nonetheless exhibit global quality failures such as
posterisation, severe desaturation, or large undifferentiated regions
arising from incomplete denoising. These failure modes are distinct from
the semantic and structural failures addressed by detection-based
validation (such as missing faces or disconnected bodies) and are not
captured by YOLO-based filtering. Aesthetic scoring provides a
complementary signal that identifies such low-quality outputs without
requiring additional semantic annotation.

Crucially, the choice of threshold determines what the aesthetic filter
admits and excludes. A threshold aligned with the training data (5.0 or
above) would reject a large proportion of generated images whose quality
is real but below the curated training distribution, introducing a
selection effect that would make the generated subset unrepresentative of
what the model actually produces. A deliberately conservative threshold
admits images that are clearly non-failed --- i.e., those with
recognisable structure, plausible colour, and sufficient detail for
downstream feature analysis --- while excluding only those outputs that are
genuinely corrupted or degenerate. The threshold of 3.0 adopted in this
work is therefore not intended to select for high aesthetic quality but to
act as a minimum viability gate, positioned well below the LAION-Aesthetics
5+ training cutoff to avoid introducing a systematic upward quality bias
into the generated subset. At this level, the filter rejects only images
whose global quality characteristics indicate a failed or incomplete
generation --- outputs that would be uninformative for downstream spurious
feature and demographic analysis regardless of their compositional content
--- while retaining the full range of structurally valid outputs the model
produces above that floor.

\subsection{Face Detection Models and Evaluation Benchmarks}

Face detection is the task of localising and classifying human faces within
an image, typically producing axis-aligned bounding boxes with associated
confidence scores. Modern face detectors are predominantly single-stage
architectures that perform classification and regression jointly in a
single forward pass, offering substantially higher throughput than
two-stage approaches while maintaining competitive accuracy on standard
benchmarks~\cite{Deng_2020_RetinaFace}.

The WIDER Face benchmark~\cite{Yang_2016_WIDERFace} is the standard
evaluation dataset for face detection research. It comprises 32,203 images
containing 393,703 annotated faces drawn from 60 event categories, spanning
a wide range of scale, pose, occlusion, and illumination conditions. Faces
are partitioned into three difficulty tiers --- Easy, Medium, and Hard ---
based on height in pixels and degree of occlusion, with the Hard partition
containing heavily occluded and very small faces that represent the upper
bound of detection difficulty. Average Precision (AP) on each partition is
the standard reported metric, with Hard AP serving as the primary
discriminator between detector architectures. For applications requiring
reliable detection of clearly visible, unoccluded faces in controlled
generative or retrieval settings --- where faces are typically well-sized
and centrally positioned --- Easy and Medium AP are the more relevant
performance indicators, as the Hard partition evaluates robustness to
conditions unlikely to arise in such contexts.

RetinaFace~\cite{Deng_2020_RetinaFace} established a strong baseline
through joint face detection and five-point landmark regression within a
Feature Pyramid Network backbone, achieving state-of-the-art WIDER Face
performance at the time of its release. SCRFD~\cite{Guo_2021_SCRFD}
introduced sample and computation redistribution to produce a family of
efficient detectors spanning a range of computational budgets, achieving
competitive Hard AP with substantially reduced FLOPs. YOLOv8-face adapts
the YOLOv8 object detection architecture~\cite{Jocher_2023_YOLOv8} to the
face detection task, inheriting its single-stage regression head and
multi-scale feature pyramid while replacing the generic class vocabulary
with face-specific anchor configurations. In practice, YOLOv8-face offers
competitive accuracy on the Easy and Medium WIDER Face partitions with
inference throughput substantially higher than RetinaFace or SCRFD,
making it well-suited for large-scale filtering pipelines where detection
speed is a primary constraint and the target faces are predominantly
well-visible.

For the purposes of this work, the relevant detection properties are
single-face precision --- the ability to correctly identify images
containing exactly one clearly visible face while rejecting images with
zero or multiple faces --- and inference throughput, since structural
filtering is applied to hundreds of thousands of candidates during
retrieval and to every generated image during the generation pipeline.
Small-face recall, the primary discriminator between detectors on the
WIDER Face Hard partition, is a secondary concern given that the
structural validation criteria explicitly require face regions of
sufficient size to support downstream FaceMesh landmark extraction.

\subsection{Facial Landmark Estimation: MediaPipe FaceMesh}

Whereas face detection localises a face region within an image, facial
landmark estimation recovers the positions of anatomically defined
keypoints within that region, enabling fine-grained spatial analysis of
face geometry. MediaPipe FaceMesh~\cite{Kartynnik_2019_FaceMesh} is a
real-time facial landmark system that predicts 468 three-dimensional face
landmarks from a single RGB image using a lightweight convolutional network
operating on a tightly cropped face region of interest. The model is
optimised for on-device inference and produces dense landmark coverage
across the full facial surface, including the eye contours, eyebrows, lips,
nose, and outer face boundary.

FaceMesh serves a functionally distinct role from face detection in
structural validation pipelines. Detection confirms the presence and
approximate location of a face; landmark estimation confirms that the
detected region contains a geometrically coherent face with sufficient
surface detail to support downstream processing. A detected bounding box
may enclose a face-like region that is too blurred, too occluded, or too
poorly rendered to yield reliable landmarks, and FaceMesh failure on such
regions provides a principled rejection criterion that a second detection
pass would not supply. The convex hull of the predicted landmarks
furthermore provides a precise polygonal mask of the facial skin region,
enabling tightly cropped face extractions that exclude surrounding hair,
clothing, or background --- a property that is directly relevant when the
extracted face region is subsequently used for skin tone classification,
where background or clothing pixels would introduce spurious colour signal
into the annotation.

This functional complementarity between bounding-box detection and
landmark-based segmentation is the basis for the two-stage face processing
pipeline adopted in this work: YOLO-based detection identifies candidate
face regions efficiently at scale, and FaceMesh landmark extraction
subsequently validates geometric coherence and produces the segmented face
crop used in downstream demographic annotation.

\section{Similarity Search in High-Dimensional Embedding Spaces}\label{subsec:faiss}

The emergence of large-scale vision-language models such as CLIP 
\cite{Radford_2021_CLIP} has established dense vector embeddings as the 
dominant representation for semantic image retrieval. CLIP embeddings 
are typically 512- or 768-dimensional floating-point vectors, and both 
queries and database entries are mapped into this shared space such that 
semantic similarity corresponds to geometric proximity. Retrieval is 
then framed as a nearest neighbour search: given a query vector, find 
the database vectors whose similarity to the query is greatest. When 
similarity is measured by cosine similarity, this reduces to finding 
vectors whose normalised inner product with the query is maximised. It 
is important to note that cosine similarity is not directly supported 
as a native distance metric in most indexing libraries; operationally, 
it is implemented by L2-normalising all vectors prior to indexing and 
then performing inner product search, since for unit vectors the inner 
product and cosine similarity are equivalent.

The central computational challenge is that naive exact search --- 
computing the similarity between a query and every vector in the 
database --- scales as $O(Nd)$, where $N$ is the number of indexed 
vectors and $d$ is the embedding dimensionality. Every query requires 
$N \times d$ multiply-accumulate operations, meaning the cost grows 
linearly with database size. For databases containing hundreds of 
millions to billions of entries, this becomes computationally 
intractable for interactive or batch retrieval workloads. Efficient 
similarity search at this scale therefore requires indexing structures 
that organise the embedding space to reduce the number of comparisons 
required per query.

\subsection*{The FAISS Library}

The Facebook AI Similarity Search (FAISS) library 
\cite{Douze_2024_FAISS}, developed by Meta AI Research, is a widely 
adopted open-source library providing a comprehensive suite of indexing 
structures and algorithms for similarity search and clustering of dense 
vectors. FAISS is designed explicitly for the high-dimensional 
floating-point vectors produced by deep learning models, and provides 
both CPU and GPU implementations. Its index types span a spectrum from 
exact to approximate nearest-neighbour search, allowing practitioners 
to navigate the trade-off between retrieval precision, query 
throughput, and memory consumption depending on the constraints of 
their application.

\subsection*{Exact Search: IndexFlatIP}

The simplest FAISS index, \texttt{IndexFlatIP}, performs exact inner 
product search with $O(Nd)$ query complexity, exhaustively computing 
the inner product between a query vector and every vector stored in the 
index. No approximation is applied --- the index guarantees that the 
true nearest neighbours are returned for every query. Despite being 
exhaustive, \texttt{IndexFlatIP} substantially outperforms 
unoptimised linear scan implementations by exploiting BLAS-level matrix 
operations and CPU SIMD (Single Instruction Multiple Data) 
acceleration, which parallelise the inner product computations across 
processor registers. When all indexed vectors are L2-normalised prior 
to insertion, the inner product between two unit vectors equals their 
cosine similarity, making \texttt{IndexFlatIP} a precise and 
computationally efficient implementation of exact cosine similarity 
search.

\subsection*{Approximate Search Methods}

For applications where the scale of the database makes even optimised 
exact search too slow, FAISS provides a range of approximate 
nearest-neighbour (ANN) index types that sacrifice retrieval exactness 
for substantially reduced query times.

\texttt{IndexIVFFlat} partitions the embedding space into $k$ Voronoi 
cells using k-means clustering, assigning each indexed vector to its 
nearest cluster centroid \cite{Jegou_2011_PQ}. At query time, only the 
vectors belonging to the $n_\text{probe}$ nearest cells to the query 
are searched, reducing query complexity from $O(Nd)$ to approximately 
$O(n_\text{probe} \cdot N/k)$. The recall of the search --- the 
proportion of true nearest neighbours successfully retrieved --- can be 
controlled by adjusting $n_\text{probe}$: higher values increase recall 
at the cost of longer query times, with $n_\text{probe} = k$ 
recovering exact search.

\texttt{IndexHNSWFlat} organises indexed vectors into a Hierarchical 
Navigable Small World (HNSW) graph \cite{Malkov_2020_HNSW}, a 
multi-layer proximity graph in which upper layers contain sparse 
long-range connections and lower layers contain dense local connections. 
Search proceeds by entering at the top layer and greedily descending 
toward the query, following edges that reduce distance at each level. 
This hierarchical traversal yields empirically logarithmic query 
complexity $O(\log N)$, meaning that doubling the database size 
increases query cost by approximately one additional traversal step 
rather than doubling the work. HNSW achieves strong recall with low 
memory overhead, making it one of the most widely used ANN structures 
in practice, though its logarithmic scaling is an empirical 
observation rather than a strict worst-case theoretical guarantee 
\cite{Malkov_2020_HNSW}.

\texttt{IndexIVFPQ} combines inverted file partitioning with Product 
Quantisation (PQ) \cite{Jegou_2011_PQ}, a lossy compression technique 
that decomposes each high-dimensional vector into $m$ subvectors and 
encodes each subvector using a learned codebook. This dramatically 
reduces the memory footprint of the index relative to storing raw 
float32 vectors, enabling billion-scale retrieval on commodity hardware, 
at the cost of quantisation error in distance computations.

\subsection*{Sources of Error in Approximate Search}

Approximate search methods introduce two orthogonal sources of 
retrieval error \cite{Douze_2024_FAISS}. The first is 
\textit{quantisation error} --- the distortion $\|x - q(x)\|$ 
introduced when a vector $x$ is replaced by its quantised 
approximation $q(x)$, which corrupts the computed similarity scores 
relative to true distances. The second is \textit{search error} --- 
the failure to visit index cells or graph nodes that contain the true 
nearest neighbours during traversal, caused by the approximate nature 
of the search strategy itself. These two error modes are orthogonal: 
quantisation error relates to the fidelity of the stored 
representations, while search error relates to the completeness of 
index traversal. For applications where retrieval correctness is 
critical --- such as when retrieved items form the empirical basis of 
a downstream analysis --- exact search eliminates both sources of error 
by guaranteeing complete traversal with no vector compression.


\section{Spurious Features in Machine Learning}
\label{sec:spurious_features}

\subsection*{Defining Spurious Features and Spurious Correlations}
A key challenge in deploying machine learning models reliably is their tendency to learn
features that are predictive within the training data but carry no causal or semantic
relationship to the target variable. Such features are termed \emph{spurious features}, and
the statistical associations they form with target labels are known as \emph{spurious correlations}~\cite{Arjovsky_2020_InvariantRiskMinimization,Sagawa_2020_DistributionallyRobustNeuralNetworksForGroupShifts}. Formally, a spurious correlation exists when a feature $f$ co-occurs with a label $y$ across the training distribution $p_{\text{train}}(f, y)$ but this co-occurrence does not persist under distribution shift, i.e., $p_{\text{train}}(f \mid y) \neq p_{\text{test}}(f \mid y)$, or
when $f$ carries no causal information about $y$ despite being statistically correlated with
it. A canonical illustration is a gender classifier trained on a dataset where women are
disproportionately photographed wearing dresses: the model may learn clothing type as a
reliable decision cue rather than facial or morphological features. This correlation holds
within the training distribution but breaks under shift, for instance when the model
encounters images of men in kilts or women in suits, exposing the feature as spurious. A
model that learns to exploit such a feature in lieu of causally relevant ones is said to
learn a \emph{shortcut}~\cite{Geirhos_2020_ShortcutLearningInDeepNeuralNetworks}.

Geirhos et al.~\cite{Geirhos_2020_ShortcutLearningInDeepNeuralNetworks} provide a
comprehensive treatment of shortcut learning in the deep learning literature. They
characterise a shortcut as any decision rule that performs well on the training distribution
by exploiting a feature correlated with the correct label but which does not reflect the
underlying task concept. Shortcuts are distinguished from genuinely useful features not by
their statistical properties within the training set but by their failure to generalise when
the spurious correlation is broken, either through natural distribution shift or deliberate
intervention. Critically, Geirhos et al. argue that shortcut learning is not a pathological
edge case but a rational consequence of empirical risk minimisation: given a choice between a
reliable spurious cue and a harder-to-learn causal feature, gradient-based optimisation will
consistently favour whichever feature reduces training loss most efficiently.

subject, the context, or the acquisition process.

At the \emph{semantic representation level}, richer image representations that preserve
object identity while discarding pixel-level detail can themselves carry dataset-specific
spurious signals across a hierarchy of abstraction. Semantic segmentation maps, which assign
a class label to every pixel, retain the spatial distribution of scene categories while
removing texture and colour, encoding information about which types of environments and
objects are systematically associated with which classes or groups. Object detection
representations, which reduce each image to a set of labelled bounding boxes, preserve
object co-occurrence statistics and coarse spatial layout while discarding all internal pixel
content. Image captions produced by vision-language models discard all visual information in
favour of natural language but still encode semantic regularities that reflect the statistical
composition of the underlying image distribution. \acrshort{vae} reconstructions, which pass
images through a learned bottleneck to compress and regenerate visual content --- as
implemented in latent diffusion architectures such as that of Rombach et
al.~\cite{Rombach_2022_CVPR} --- provide a further diagnostic: if reconstructed images
remain discriminable after compression, the bias resides primarily in semantic content rather
than in low-level acquisition artefacts such as jpeg compression or sensor noise.
Taken together, these representation types demonstrate that spurious features are not
confined to the pixel domain but permeate the full hierarchy of visual abstraction.

\subsection*{Spurious Features and Demographic Bias}

The intersection of spurious features and demographic fairness constitutes a particularly
consequential research area.  When a spurious feature correlates not merely with a label but
with a sensitive attribute such as gender, race, or skin tone, a model that exploits the
shortcut will systematically produce disparate outcomes across demographic groups.  Buolamwini
and Gebru~\cite{Buolamwini_2018_BiasGenderClassification} provide a foundational empirical
demonstration of this: commercial gender classifiers trained on datasets overrepresenting
lighter-skinned individuals exhibit substantially higher error rates for darker-skinned
individuals, a disparity attributable in part to the model relying on skin-tone correlates
as classification shortcuts.  Mehrabi et al.~\cite{Mehrabi_2022_SurveyBiasFairnessMachine}
offer a comprehensive survey cataloguing the mechanisms by which such demographic shortcuts
enter models, the most relevant of which being representation bias (some groups being underrepresented in
training data), measurement bias (features being measured differently across groups) and
aggregation bias (heterogeneous sub-populations being pooled under a single model).

Critically, even features that appear neutral --- colour statistics, pose distributions,
background scenes --- can function as proxies for demographic attributes when the
dataset composition creates systematic correlations between these low-level properties
and group membership.  Meister et al.~\cite{Meister_2023_GenderArtifactsinVisualDatasets}
term these \emph{gender artifacts} and demonstrate that they are effectively impossible to
fully remove from standard visual datasets without also removing information necessary for
downstream tasks.  This proxy mechanism means that standard debiasing interventions which
remove explicit demographic signals may leave intact the spurious low-level features that
encode equivalent information, producing only superficial corrections.

\subsection*{Spurious Features in Text-to-Image Generative Systems}

The concern about spurious features takes on a distinct character in the context of
text-to-image generative models.  Whereas discriminative models learn to \emph{exploit}
spurious correlations to make predictions, generative models trained on biased data learn to
\emph{reproduce} those correlations in their outputs: biased statistical regularities present
in the training corpus become embedded in the model's learned prior over images, causing the
model to synthesise spurious feature patterns even when the conditioning prompt contains no
information that should elicit them.

The training pipeline of latent diffusion models such as Stable
Diffusion~\cite{Rombach_2022_CVPR} illustrates this concretely.  The model learns a joint
distribution over image latents conditioned on text embeddings derived from CLIP~\cite{Radford_2021_CLIP}.
Since CLIP itself is pretrained on web-scraped image-text pairs, it encodes the same
societal and compositional biases present in internet imagery~\cite{Birhane_2021_MultiModalDatasetsMisogynyPornography,
	Schuhmann_2022_Laion5b}.  Spurious correlations in the training data between text tokens
(e.g.\ occupation words) and low-level image features (e.g.\ skin tone distributions, colour
palettes, or compositional conventions) are absorbed into the diffusion model's score
function, causing those features to be hallucinated at generation time even in the absence
of explicit demographic prompting.

Bianchi et al.~\cite{Bianchi_2023_TTIGenAmplifiesDemographicStereotypes} demonstrate this
empirically, showing that text-to-image models not only reflect the demographic
under-representation present in their training data but actively amplify stereotypical
associations: prompts specifying occupations produce generated images with demographic
distributions more extreme than those documented in real-world labour statistics.  Luccioni
et al.~\cite{Luccioni_2023_AnalyzingSocietalRepresentationsDiffusionModels} extend this
finding across a broader set of models and prompt categories, confirming that the amplification
effect is systemic rather than model-specific.  Critically, neither study addresses the
question of \emph{which specific low-level image features} encode these biases, treating the
model as a black box and measuring only demographic outcomes.

\subsection*{Understanding Bias in Large-Scale Visual Datasets}
\label{subsec:zeng2024}

Zeng et al.~\cite{Zeng_2024_UnderstandingBiasinLargeScaleVisualDatasets} aim to identify
the concrete forms of bias that make large-scale pre-training datasets easily distinguishable
by neural networks. Their work builds on the dataset classification task introduced by
Torralba and Efros~\cite{Torralba_2011_UnbiasedLookDatasetBias} in 2011 and revisited by
Liu and He~\cite{Liu_2024_DecadeBattleDatasetBias} in 2024, who found that three widely-used
datasets --- YFCC100M~\cite{Thomee_2016_YFCC100M}, CC12M~\cite{Changpinyo_2021_CC12M}, and
DataComp-1B~\cite{Gadre_2023_DataComp} --- can be classified by a neural network with
84.7\,\% accuracy. While this confirms the datasets are biased, it does not explain
\emph{which} image properties are responsible. Zeng et al.\ address this gap.

\subsubsection*{Transformation-Based Bias Quantification}

The central contribution is a transformation-based framework. The idea is straightforward:
apply a transformation that preserves one type of visual information while removing all
others, train a dataset classifier on the transformed images, and use the resulting accuracy
to measure how much bias that type of information carries. The three datasets --- YFCC100M,
CC12M, and DataComp-1B, referred to collectively as YCD --- represent the kind of large-scale
internet-scraped data used to pre-train modern vision models. For each transformation, 1M
images per dataset are used for training and 10K for validation using a ConvNeXt-Tiny model
trained for 30 epochs. On unmodified images, Zeng et al.\ achieve a reference accuracy of
82.0\,\%, which all transformation results are compared against.

The transformations cover the full range of visual information from raw pixels to high-level
semantics. At the semantic level, segmentation using ViT-Adapter-Large with the ADE20K
vocabulary (150 classes) converts each image into a colour-coded class map, removing texture
but keeping the spatial layout of objects; the classifier reaches 69.8\,\% accuracy. Object
detection using ViTDet-Huge with the LVIS vocabulary (1,203 classes) keeps bounding boxes
and class labels but removes all pixel content, yielding 61.9\,\%. Image captioning with
LLaVA\,1.5 removes all visual information entirely, yet short captions still achieve
63.8\,\% and long captions 66.1\,\%, both close to the segmentation result. These results
show that semantic content --- specifically, which categories of objects appear in each
dataset --- is a primary source of dataset bias.

A \acrshort{vae} experiment provides further context. Passing images through the bottleneck
of a pretrained Stable Diffusion \acrshort{vae} and reconstructing them only reduces accuracy
from 82.0\,\% to 77.4\,\%, showing that low-level properties such as jpeg
compression artefacts or resolution differences contribute relatively little to the overall
bias.

The structural transformations produce a surprising result. Canny edge detection achieves
71.0\,\% accuracy, \acrshort{sam}-based class-agnostic contour extraction reaches 73.2\,\%,
and monocular depth estimation with Depth-Anything-V2 yields 73.1\,\%. These representations
contain no object labels or semantic information, yet they \emph{outperform} most semantic
transformations. This shows that the shapes and spatial arrangements of objects differ
significantly across datasets and carry as much discriminative information as object category
--- an important finding for any bias mitigation strategy focused solely on semantic content.

To separate the contribution of local structure from global spatial layout, Zeng et al.\
run pixel-level and patch-level shuffling experiments. Randomly shuffling individual pixels
removes all spatial structure and limits the classifier to colour statistics alone; accuracy
falls to 52.2\,\%, just above the 33.3\,\% chance level. Shuffling $16\!\times\!16$ patches
instead recovers nearly all discriminability, reaching 81.2\,\%. This shows that local
spatial structure within small image regions is sufficient to distinguish datasets, and that
global composition matters much less than local texture and shape. A mean-RGB experiment
pushes this further by compressing each image to a single average colour per channel.
Even this three-number summary achieves 48.5\,\% accuracy, roughly 15 percentage points
above chance. YFCC images are substantially darker on average than CC or DataComp, likely
because YFCC contains more outdoor photography captured under varied natural lighting.

Frequency domain analysis shows that bias is present across the full frequency spectrum.
Applying high-pass filters (capturing texture and sharp edges) yields 79.2\,\% accuracy,
while low-pass filters (capturing smooth gradients and global structure) yield 70.4\,\%,
both close to the reference. This means that frequency-domain spurious signals exist at
multiple scales simultaneously, and that mitigation approaches targeting a single frequency
range will not remove the bias entirely.

Finally, combining two transformations together increases accuracy beyond what either
achieves alone. For example, combining pixel shuffling with SAM contours reaches 74.2\,\%,
compared to 73.1\,\% for SAM contours alone. This shows that different types of spurious
signals carry partly independent information, which is directly relevant to the
multi-transformation approach used in this thesis.

\subsubsection*{Propagation of Bias into Generative Models}

A central question for this thesis is whether biases in training data carry through into the
outputs of generative models. Zeng et al.\ test this by training separate unconditional
Diffusion Transformer (DiT) models on each of the three YCD datasets and classifying the
resulting synthetic images. These generated images can be classified with 77.6\,\% accuracy,
nearly matching the 82.0\,\% reference on real images. This demonstrates that generative
models reproduce the distributional biases of their training data to a degree that is largely
preserved in their outputs. A second experiment using a single pretrained SDXL-Turbo model
conditioned on LLaVA-generated captions achieves 58.1\,\%, confirming that semantic bias is
partly transmitted even when the generative model is not dataset-specific. Together, these
results show a clear link between training data composition and generative model output,
directly supporting the central hypothesis of this thesis.

\subsubsection*{Semantic Explanation of Dataset Differences}

To explain \emph{why} the datasets are distinguishable, Zeng et al.\ use two complementary
approaches. First, Gradient-weighted Class Activation Mapping (\acrshort{gradcam}) applied
to the reference classifier shows that the model focuses on specific objects --- such as
elephant herds, tables, and pens --- to identify dataset origin. This motivates the use of
object-level analysis as an interpretability tool.

A logistic regression model trained on binary object-presence vectors from the ImageNet,
LVIS, and ADE20K vocabularies achieves 52.0--52.4\,\% accuracy depending on the vocabulary,
well above the 33.3\,\% chance level but well below the 82.0\,\% of the neural classifier.
This gap shows that object identity alone does not fully explain the neural classifier's
ability to distinguish datasets, meaning that lower-level and structural signals also play a
role. The regression coefficients reveal clear patterns: YFCC is associated with outdoor
infrastructure such as traffic lights, poles, and stages, while CC and DataComp are
associated with household items, products, and digital graphics. YFCC also contains a
notably higher variety of objects per image than the other two datasets, consistent with its
origin in less-curated, user-generated Flickr photography.

Open-ended language analysis using \acrshort{lda} topic modelling and GPT-4o summarisation
of LLaVA-generated captions characterises YFCC as outdoor and human-centred, DataComp as
product-focused with clean backgrounds and few people, and CC as a mix of the two. These
findings are validated by prompting LLaVA to answer simple binary questions --- such as
``Is this image a product showcase?'' and ``Is this image depicting an outdoor scene?'' ---
across all three datasets, confirming that the identified differences reflect genuine
properties of the data rather than quirks of the analysis method.


\subsection*{Evaluation of Image Captioning Quality}

The transformation-based framework of Zeng et al.\ requires that 
generated captions faithfully reflect image content: a captioning 
model that produces degenerate, repetitive, or semantically 
inaccurate descriptions would corrupt the semantic signal the 
transformation is designed to preserve, and any downstream 
classification result would measure caption failure rather than 
dataset-level bias. Selecting an appropriate captioning model 
therefore requires a principled evaluation protocol. The literature 
has developed two complementary families of metrics for this 
purpose: reference-based metrics that compare generated captions 
to human-authored ground truths, and reference-free metrics that 
assess alignment between a generated caption and its corresponding 
image directly.

\paragraph{Reference-Based N-gram Metrics}
The earliest and most widely adopted caption evaluation metric is 
BLEU (Bilingual Evaluation Understudy), introduced by Papineni et 
al.~\cite{Papineni_2002_BLEU} for machine translation and 
subsequently adopted for image captioning evaluation. BLEU computes 
a modified n-gram precision score between the generated text and 
one or more reference strings, penalised by a brevity factor to 
discourage degenerate short outputs. The BLEU@$n$ variant measures 
precision up to $n$-grams, with BLEU@4 most commonly reported as 
it rewards longer matching phrase sequences. A fundamental 
limitation of BLEU is its insensitivity to synonymy and 
morphological variation: a caption that expresses the correct 
meaning through different but valid vocabulary receives no credit 
for any n-gram that does not appear verbatim in the reference. This 
makes BLEU a poor proxy for human judgement in settings where 
lexical flexibility is common.

METEOR (Metric for Evaluation of Translation with Explicit 
ORdering) was developed by Banerjee and 
Lavie~\cite{Banerjee_2005_METEOR} specifically to address this 
limitation. METEOR aligns generated and reference captions using 
exact token matches, stemmed matches, and synonym matches via 
WordNet, computing a weighted harmonic mean of precision and recall 
over the resulting alignment. Empirical studies consistently show 
that METEOR correlates more strongly with human judgements of 
caption quality than BLEU, particularly for captions that 
paraphrase reference content rather than reproduce it 
lexically~\cite{Banerjee_2005_METEOR}.

CIDEr (Consensus-based Image Description Evaluation) was 
introduced by Vedantam et al.~\cite{Vedantam_2015_CIDEr} to 
address a further limitation of both BLEU and METEOR: their 
indifference to the informativeness of matched n-grams. CIDEr 
computes TF-IDF weighted cosine similarity between n-gram frequency 
vectors of the generated and reference captions, where the IDF 
weight is computed across the full evaluation corpus. This down-weights 
common n-grams that appear across many captions --- such as 
\textit{a man} or \textit{an image of} --- and up-weights 
distinctive n-grams that are specific to particular images. CIDEr 
has been shown to achieve the highest correlation with human 
consensus judgements of any single n-gram metric on standard 
benchmarks~\cite{Vedantam_2015_CIDEr}.

\paragraph{Diversity as a Quality Signal}
N-gram precision metrics are insensitive to a failure mode common 
in sequence generation models: template collapse, in which a model 
learns to produce a small set of high-frequency output patterns 
that achieve acceptable n-gram overlap with references while 
generating almost no variation across inputs. A model exhibiting 
this behaviour may achieve competitive BLEU scores while producing 
outputs that are practically useless for any application requiring 
content-grounded descriptions. Distinct-$n$ diversity, defined as 
the ratio of unique $n$-grams to total $n$-grams across all 
generated captions, provides a complementary diagnostic that 
directly quantifies vocabulary collapse~\cite{Li_2016_DiversityNMT}. 
A low diversity score indicates that the model's output 
distribution has collapsed to a small set of templates, 
irrespective of what n-gram overlap metrics report. For 
large-scale captioning applications, where the full diversity of 
image content must be reflected in the generated descriptions, 
diversity metrics are therefore a necessary complement to 
precision-based evaluation.

\paragraph{Semantic Similarity via Sentence Embeddings}
A further limitation shared by all n-gram metrics is their 
surface-level operationalisation of meaning: two captions can be 
semantically equivalent while sharing few or no n-grams, and two 
captions can share many n-grams while being semantically 
contradictory. Sentence-BERT (SBERT), introduced by Reimers and 
Gurevych~\cite{Reimers_2019_SBERT}, addresses this by encoding 
sentences into a dense embedding space using a siamese 
fine-tuned BERT architecture, where semantic similarity is measured 
as cosine distance between embeddings. Because the embedding space 
is trained to place paraphrases near one another regardless of 
surface form, SBERT similarity provides a paraphrase-robust 
measure of meaning preservation that is complementary to n-gram 
overlap. In caption evaluation, SBERT cosine similarity between 
generated and reference captions captures whether the semantic 
content of the description is correct even when it is expressed 
through different vocabulary.

\paragraph{Reference-Free Visual Grounding: CLIPScore and RefCLIP-S}
All reference-based metrics share a structural assumption that 
limits their applicability: they presuppose that the reference 
captions are exhaustive, so that a correct generated caption will 
necessarily achieve high similarity to at least one reference. In 
practice, human reference captions cover only a subset of the 
valid descriptions of any image. Hessel et 
al.~\cite{Hessel_2021_CLIPScore} propose CLIPScore as a 
reference-free alternative that evaluates image-caption alignment 
directly using a pretrained CLIP 
model~\cite{Radford_2021_CLIP}. 
CLIP-S computes the cosine similarity between the CLIP image 
embedding and the CLIP text embedding of the generated caption, 
scaled to the range $[0, 2.5]$. Because CLIP is trained to align 
images and their natural language descriptions across a large 
web-scraped corpus, a high CLIP-S score indicates that the 
generated caption is grounded in the visual content of the image 
regardless of whether it matches the reference phrasing. Hessel 
et al.\ demonstrate that CLIP-S correlates strongly with human 
judgements of caption quality, and in several settings 
outperforms reference-based metrics when the reference captions 
are sparse or stylistically narrow.

RefCLIP-S extends this by combining image grounding with reference 
similarity. It is defined as the harmonic mean of CLIP-S and 
RefSim, where RefSim is the maximum CLIP text-text cosine 
similarity between the generated caption and any reference caption 
for the same image. This formulation penalises captions that are 
visually grounded but semantically distant from any human 
description, and also penalises captions that match reference 
phrasing but are poorly grounded in the image content. Hessel et 
al.\ demonstrate that RefCLIP-S achieves higher correlation with 
human judgements than either component alone, making it the most 
diagnostic single metric available for captioning evaluation when 
both visual fidelity and semantic coherence are required.

Taken together, these metrics form a complementary evaluation 
battery. N-gram metrics assess surface-level lexical overlap; 
METEOR and SBERT extend this to synonymy and semantic equivalence; 
diversity metrics detect output collapse invisible to precision 
measures; and CLIPScore and RefCLIP-S provide reference-free 
visual grounding that is robust to reference sparsity. No single 
metric is sufficient, and the failure modes of each motivate the 
multi-metric evaluation protocol employed in this thesis.

\subsection*{Gender Artifacts in Visual Datasets}
\label{subsec:meister2023}
% -----------------------------------------------------------------

Meister et al.~\cite{Meister_2023_GenderArtifactsinVisualDatasets} systematically
investigate the nature and prevalence of \emph{gender artifacts} in large-scale computer
vision datasets.  A gender artifact is defined as a visual cue that is both \emph{learnable}
--- a modern image classifier can use it to distinguish images labelled female from those
labelled male --- and \emph{interpretable}, meaning it has a recognisable human corollary
such as colour, shape, or scene composition.  This dual constraint focuses the analysis on
the most consequential correlations: those that automated systems can exploit and that carry
socially legible meaning.

The work is motivated by a gap in the fairness literature.  A widely used family of
bias-mitigation techniques, collectively termed \emph{fairness through blindness}, assumes
that removing a person's visible appearance from an image is sufficient to eliminate the
gendered signal.  Meister et al.\ challenge this directly by asking a more fundamental
question: to what extent can gendered information truly be removed from a visual dataset?

One methodological point is important for interpreting the results.  The authors call their
model a \emph{gender artifact model} rather than a gender classifier, because it is a
diagnostic tool for understanding dataset correlations, not a system intended to predict
gender identity.  The gender labels used are derived from external annotators' perceptions
rather than self-reported identity, reflecting the limitations of existing dataset annotation
practices, and are used solely to reveal distributional differences in the data.

\subsubsection*{Datasets and Model}

The analysis focuses on two widely used object-recognition benchmarks: Microsoft
COCO~\cite{Lin_2014_COCO} and OpenImages~\cite{Kuznetsova_2020_OpenImagesV4}.  For COCO,
gender labels are inferred from image captions using a keyword-matching heuristic following
prior work~\cite{Zhao_2017_MenAlsoLikeShopping}; for OpenImages, labels come from the More
Inclusive Annotations for People (MIAP) subset~\cite{Schumann_2021_StepInclusivePeopleAnnotations}.
Both datasets are skewed male, with 69.2\,\% and 62.9\,\% male-labelled images in COCO and
OpenImages respectively.  The gender artifact model is a
ResNet-50~\cite{He_2016_DeepResidualLearning} pretrained on ImageNet, with hyperparameters
tuned on a held-out validation set.  Performance is reported as the area under the ROC
curve (AUC), where 50\,\% represents chance-level discrimination.

\subsubsection*{Resolution and Colour Artifacts}

The first set of experiments investigates artifacts in the global perceptual properties of
images.  Images are progressively downsampled from $224\!\times\!224$ to $7\!\times\!7$
pixels, with the model trained and evaluated at each resolution separately.  In colour
images, the AUC remains well above chance even at $7\!\times\!7$ (59.2\,\% on COCO;
63.9\,\% on OpenImages), a resolution at which almost all semantic content is destroyed.
Repeating the experiment in greyscale, the AUC falls to near chance at $28\!\times\!28$,
showing that colour is the primary carrier of gender-correlated signal at low resolutions,
and that the model's ability to discriminate cannot be attributed to residual shape or object
information.

To isolate this colour artifact in its simplest form, the authors train a logistic regression
classifier on just three features: the mean pixel value of the red, green, and blue channels.
This achieves AUCs of 58.0\,\% $\pm$\,0.4 and 59.1\,\% $\pm$\,0.4 on COCO and OpenImages
respectively.  The likely explanation is a compositional confound: images of male-labelled
individuals are disproportionately set in outdoor sports contexts dominated by green tones,
while images of female-labelled individuals more often feature close-up portraits with
lighter average pixel values.

\subsubsection*{Person and Background Artifacts}

The second set of experiments uses a systematic occlusion protocol to separate gender
artifacts arising from the person from those arising from the background.  The conditions
evaluated are: the full unmodified image (\emph{Full}); the full image with the background
masked in black (\emph{Full NoBg}); the image with the person replaced by their segmentation
mask (\emph{MaskSegm}); the image with the person replaced by a bounding box
(\emph{MaskRect}); and combinations of these with and without the background.

\paragraph{Person appearance.}
With all person pixels visible, the gender artifact model achieves an AUC of 93.4\,\%
$\pm$\,0.2 on COCO.  Removing the background reduces this only slightly, to 92.7\,\%
$\pm$\,0.2, confirming that the person's appearance is the dominant signal under normal
conditions.

\paragraph{Person shape and pose.}
When the person's appearance is replaced by a white segmentation mask but the background
remains visible (MaskSegm), the model still achieves 79.6\,\% $\pm$\,0.3 on COCO.  The
silhouette alone --- which still reveals cues such as hair length and clothing outline ---
carries substantial gendered information even before texture or colour are considered.
Removing the background as well (MaskSegm NoBg), performance drops only to 74.8\,\%
$\pm$\,0.3, confirming that silhouette shape and position are meaningful gender artifacts
independent of scene context.

Taking this further, the authors train the model on the 17 COCO keypoint locations alone,
with no pixel content.  This yields an AUC of 64.8\,\% $\pm$\,0.4.  A normalised pose-only
representation --- keypoints rescaled to a fixed area and centred to remove size and location
information --- still achieves 58.0\,\% $\pm$\,0.0, establishing body pose as a gender
artifact in its own right.  Qualitatively, male-associated poses tend to be smaller and
active (e.g.\ sporting actions), while female-associated poses tend to be larger relative to
the frame and static.  This is supported quantitatively: the normalised area of
female-labelled persons in the COCO training set (0.18 $\pm$\,$10^{-4}$) is significantly
larger than for male-labelled persons (0.13 $\pm$\,$10^{-5}$), confirmed at $p < 0.01$ and
replicated in OpenImages.

\paragraph{Background as a gender artifact.}
When the person is occluded but the background is left intact, the gendered signal is not
eliminated.  Under the MaskRect condition --- matching the occlusion strategy used by several
prominent mitigation methods~\cite{Burns_2018_WomenAlsoSnowboard,Wang_2019_BalancedDatasetsNotEnough}
--- the AUC remains at 70.8\,\% $\pm$\,0.3 on COCO and 63.1\,\% $\pm$\,0.4 on OpenImages.
This shows that the background scene itself encodes gender-correlated information, which
directly undermines the core assumption of fairness-through-blindness approaches.

Class Activation Map (CAM) analysis explains why: when the person is occluded, the model
shifts its attention onto background objects, associating indoor objects (e.g.\ ovens and
beds) with female labels and outdoor objects (e.g.\ skateboards and motorcycles) with male
labels.  Scene type, not just pixel statistics, is the operative artifact in these
conditions.

Out-of-distribution evaluations --- training on COCO and testing on OpenImages, and vice
versa --- confirm that these patterns hold across datasets, suggesting they reflect broad
regularities in how photographs are composed rather than quirks of any single collection
process.

\subsubsection*{Contextual Object Artifacts}

The third set of experiments examines the gender information carried by the identity of
objects in a scene.  A logistic regression classifier trained on a binary object-presence
vector achieves an AUC of 75.4\,\% on COCO and 63.3\,\% on OpenImages.  The classifier's
weights reflect familiar stereotypes: objects most predictive of female-labelled images
include hair driers, handbags, and teddy bears, while those most predictive of male-labelled
images include skateboards, ties, and snowboards.

An iterative removal experiment shows that this gendered information is not concentrated in a
few obviously gendered objects.  Retaining only the 14 most gendered objects still achieves
72.1\,\% AUC.  Random removal tells the same story: 30 randomly selected objects from the
80 annotated COCO categories yield 66.8\,\%, and just 10 randomly chosen objects still
achieve 58.0\,\%.  Gendered information is distributed broadly across the object vocabulary,
and there is no small set of objects whose removal would meaningfully eliminate object-level
artifacts.

\subsubsection*{Analysis of Fairness-Through-Blindness Methods}

To evaluate how existing mitigation methods interact with these artifacts in practice, the
authors apply the adversarial debiasing approach of Wang et
al.~\cite{Wang_2019_BalancedDatasetsNotEnough} to COCO images.  This method trains a U-Net to
identify and remove image pixels that carry salient gender information.  When the authors
examine which pixels are actually removed from held-out validation images, the results are
striking: 76.8\,\% of removed pixels come from the background, not the person, and 20.4\,\%
$\pm$\,15.7 of all background pixels across the dataset are masked out.  The objects most
frequently affected include giraffes, trains, elephants, buses, and horses --- objects
commonly found in the active outdoor scenes associated with male-labelled images.

This shows that the method ends up suppressing scene-level information --- including objects
that may be necessary for downstream tasks such as object recognition --- rather than the
person-centric gendered information it was designed to target.  The broader lesson is that
because gendered signals are spread throughout the image, any method trying to remove them
will inevitably also remove task-relevant content.

\subsubsection*{Discussion and Broader Implications}

The authors draw three main conclusions.  First, gender artifacts are so widely distributed
across image structure --- from mean colour to silhouette shape to object co-occurrence ---
that removing them without also removing task-relevant information is not feasible.  Second,
the experiments reveal an incoherence in what a gender classifier actually predicts: if a
model can infer gender labels from the mean pixel colour, a 7$\times$7 blurred image, or the
presence of a skateboard, then what it has learned reflects the compositional biases of the
dataset rather than gender expression in any meaningful sense.  Third, the authors recommend
moving away from fairness-through-blindness approaches towards \emph{fairness-aware} methods
that explicitly account for distributional differences across demographic groups, and towards
disaggregated evaluation practices that make those differences visible.

\section{Demographic Annotation in Large-Scale Image Datasets}

To study demographic bias in large-scale visual datasets and generative models, images must be annotated with sensitive attributes such as gender and skin tone. These annotations are the variable against which spurious feature distributions are measured, so the quality and validity of the annotation process is a central methodological concern. Unlike object category labels, where a clear ground truth can be defined in principle, demographic annotations are contested. They sit at the intersection of biological, social, perceptual, and cultural factors, which makes them inherently harder to assign reliably. This section surveys the annotation practices used in large-scale computer vision research, examines the sources of measurement bias and label uncertainty in both human and automated annotation, reviews the main frameworks used for skin tone annotation, outlines the core limitations of visual demographic inference, and addresses the specific challenges that arise when annotating synthetic images produced by generative models.


\subsection{Human and Automated Annotation Practices}
Demographic attributes in visual datasets have traditionally been labelled by human annotators. Early face recognition datasets such as Labelled Faces in the Wild (LFW) \cite{Huang_2007_LFW} included identity labels and exhibited variation in attributes such as gender and race, but were not designed explicitly for demographic fairness analysis. Large-scale object-centric benchmarks such as MS-COCO \cite{Lin_2014_COCO} include the category “person,” yet do not provide structured demographic annotations. OpenImages \cite{Kuznetsova_2020_OpenImagesV4} extends this hierarchy by including subclasses such as man, woman, boy, and girl, but these annotations were not originally developed under a fairness-driven protocol and are not exhaustive. A more explicit attempt at inclusive demographic annotation is the More Inclusive Annotations for People (MIAP) dataset \cite{Schumann_2021_StepInclusivePeopleAnnotations}, which revises the OpenImages person hierarchy and introduces non-binary gender presentation labels under a formal multi-annotator framework designed to support fairness analysis. Considered together, these efforts demonstrate that human annotation of demographic attributes is feasible at scale, but methodologically complex. Unlike object category labels, demographic attributes are highly sensitive to annotator subjectivity, cultural background, and perceptual ambiguity, making disagreement and label uncertainty intrinsic rather than incidental to the task.

Inter-annotator agreement for demographic labels is typically lower than for object category labels, reflecting the greater interpretive ambiguity involved in assigning socially constructed attributes. Studies of automated gender classification systems show that performance degrades most sharply for individuals whose gender presentation does not conform to binary or culturally dominant norms \cite{Scheuerman_2019_HowComputersSeeGender}. This pattern suggests that ambiguity and non-conformity pose systematic challenges for both human and machine labeling processes. Crucially, such disagreement cannot be treated as mere annotation noise. Aroyo and Welty \cite{Aroyo_2015_CrowdTruth} argue that annotator disagreement often encodes genuine differences in perception and interpretation, and that collapsing annotations to a single majority label can obscure meaningful variation in how examples are understood.

For skin tone annotation, reliability is further complicated by the fact that visible appearance depends not only on intrinsic pigmentation but also on imaging conditions. Clinical and web-sourced image datasets frequently vary in illumination, camera calibration, and post-processing, making it difficult to disentangle perceived skin tone from lighting artefacts \cite{Groh_2021_Fitzpatrick17k}. As a result, both perceptual ambiguity and technical variability contribute to uncertainty in demographic labels.

At the scale of web-scraped datasets such as Re-LAION-5B, manual demographic annotation is not practically feasible. As a result, large-scale studies frequently rely on automated demographic inference, in which pre-trained classifiers are applied to images to estimate attributes such as gender, perceived race, age, or skin tone. Datasets and models such as FairFace \cite{Karkkainen_2021_FairFace}, which provides race, gender, and age labels across a relatively balanced distribution of seven racial categories, are commonly used for this purpose due to their accessibility and improved demographic coverage relative to earlier benchmarks.

A purpose-built benchmark for evaluating automated demographic classifiers
at scale is the Casual Conversations v2 (CCv2) dataset~\cite{Porgali_2023_CCv2}.
CCv2 consists of over 26,000 video clips featuring more than 5,000
consenting participants recorded across a range of lighting conditions,
indoor and outdoor environments, and camera setups. Crucially, demographic
labels in CCv2 were provided directly by the participants through a
structured self-reporting process covering self-identified gender,
age, language and dialect, disability status, physical attributes,
and geo-location. In addition to these self-reported attributes,
trained annotators labelled apparent skin tone using both the
Fitzpatrick Skin Type and Monk Skin Tone scales. This dual-annotation
design separates self-identified attributes from perceived attributes,
providing a principled basis for evaluating the degree to which automated
classifiers capture perceived appearance rather than identity. The dataset
was constructed with substantial demographic diversity across skin tone,
age, and environment to support stratified performance evaluation, making
it a well-suited benchmark for assessing whether gender and skin tone
classifiers generalise beyond the dominant lighter-skinned, studio-lit
distributions on which many face analysis systems were originally trained.

A complementary benchmark is the Fairness in Computer Vision Evaluation
(FACET) dataset~\cite{Gustafson_2023_FACET}, which was developed specifically
to support structured auditing of computer vision models across a range of
demographic attributes. FACET provides annotations for approximately
32,000 images drawn from the Segment Anything 1 Billion (SA-1B) dataset,
covering perceived gender presentation, perceived skin tone using the Monk
Skin Tone (MST) scale, perceived age group, hair color and hair type, and
several additional attributes relevant to fairness evaluation. Annotations
were produced by trained human raters under a structured multi-stage
annotation pipeline, with perceived skin tone labels aggregated across
multiple annotators to capture the distribution of annotator judgments.
These rich annotations enable systematic evaluation of model performance
across demographic attributes and their intersections. Because FACET
includes exhaustive annotations for both demographic attributes and
person-related classes alongside bounding boxes and segmentation masks,
it supports fairness evaluation across multiple vision tasks including
classification, detection, and segmentation.

However, automated annotation introduces a structural circularity. Demographic classifiers are themselves trained on human-annotated datasets that encode the same perceptual subjectivities, cultural assumptions, and representational imbalances discussed above. As demonstrated by Buolamwini and Gebru \cite{Buolamwini_2018_GenderShades}, performance disparities in commercial gender classifiers reflect underlying dataset imbalances and phenotypic skew. When such models are subsequently used to annotate new datasets, they propagate the statistical structure and potential biases of their training data into downstream analyses.

Automated demographic inference therefore does not eliminate the epistemic limitations of manual annotation; rather, it relocates them into a learned decision boundary whose internal criteria are opaque. At scale, this can transform annotation bias from an observable human disagreement into a systematic, model-mediated measurement artefact.

\subsection{Measurement Bias and Label Uncertainty}

Measurement bias arises when the process used to assign labels systematically distorts the metric it intends to measure \cite{Mehrabi_2022_SurveyBiasFairnessMachine}. In the context of demographic annotation, this distortion can occur at multiple stages: through annotator subjectivity, dataset imbalance, or automated inference error. Unlike object categories, demographic attributes such as gender presentation or skin tone lack stable visual ground truth and are instead inferred from perceptual cues. As a result, measurement error is not purely random but often structured.

Empirical audits of commercial gender classifiers demonstrate that error rates vary substantially across intersectional subgroups. Buolamwini and Gebru \cite{Buolamwini_2018_GenderShades} show that darker-skinned females experience significantly higher misclassification rates than lighter-skinned males, revealing systematic disparities tied to both observable traits and dataset composition. When automated classifiers trained on such data are used to annotate large-scale web datasets, these subgroup-specific error patterns become embedded in the generated labels.

In large-scale auditing pipelines, confidence thresholds are often used to discard low-probability predictions. While this improves precision, it can also disproportionately exclude images whose appearance deviates from dominant training distributions. Because datasets such as FairFace were explicitly constructed to mitigate racial imbalance \cite{Karkkainen_2021_FairFace}, their existence highlights the fact that earlier benchmarks were systematically skewed. If annotation models inherit residual imbalances, confidence filtering may amplify rather than eliminate selection effects.

These issues are particularly consequential when demographic labels function not as outcomes but as covariates against which feature distributions are measured. In such settings, structured annotation error can produce apparent correlations between visual artefacts and demographic attributes that reflect classifier error patterns rather than properties of the underlying data. Without ground truth labels at scale, disentangling genuine distributional structure from measurement artefact becomes statistically intractable. Careful selection and documentation of annotation models is therefore essential when demographic attributes are used to audit spurious correlations in large-scale visual datasets.

\subsection{Skin Tone Annotation Frameworks}

The choice of skin tone annotation framework is a methodological decision that directly affects the validity of downstream fairness and correlation analyses. A skin tone scale must satisfy two criteria for use in large-scale visual auditing: (i) construct validity for image-based measurement, and (ii) operational stability under real-world imaging conditions.

The most widely used skin tone framework in computer vision is the Fitzpatrick Skin Type (FST) scale. Originally developed in dermatology to classify sun-reactive skin response to ultraviolet exposure \cite{Fitzpatrick_1988_SkinTypes}, the six-point scale was not designed to measure visual pigmentation per se, but rather tanning and burning propensity. Its categories are anchored in self-reported photoreactivity rather than directly observable colour properties, creating a construct mismatch when applied to image-based demographic annotation. Subsequent work has documented both the underrepresentation of darker skin types in dermatological datasets and the compression of variation at the darker end of the Fitzpatrick scale \cite{Groh_2021_Fitzpatrick17k}. In fairness auditing contexts, such compression reduces sensitivity precisely where subgroup disparities are often most pronounced. The scale’s clinical origin and uneven granularity therefore limit its suitability as a measurement instrument for large-scale image datasets.

The Monk Skin Tone (MST) scale was developed to address these limitations by providing a ten-point ordinal framework explicitly designed for technology and machine learning applications \cite{Schumann_2024_MonkSkinTone}. Unlike Fitzpatrick, the MST scale is anchored to visual reference swatches intended for photographic assessment rather than sun-reactive history. Empirical annotation studies show that trained annotators can reliably apply MST categories in alignment with expert judgments, although systematic variation across annotator groups remains observable \cite{Schumann_2024_MonkSkinTone}. This highlights an important methodological point: while MST improves construct alignment for image-based tasks, skin tone annotation remains a subjective perceptual process rather than an objective measurement.

Perceived inclusivity also matters when demographic attributes are used for fairness evaluation. A large-scale survey comparing Fitzpatrick, the Monk scale, and a 40-shade commercial palette found that Fitzpatrick was perceived as significantly less representative, particularly among darker-skinned and historically marginalized participants \cite{Heldreth_2024_WhichSkinToneMeasures}. Notably, the ten-point MST scale was perceived as comparably inclusive to the much finer-grained commercial palette, suggesting that improved colour distribution and category design can increase representativeness without excessive cognitive burden. These findings support the adoption of MST as a more appropriate perceptual measurement instrument for fairness-oriented annotation.

A separate modelling decision concerns how the MST categories are treated analytically. Although skin tone varies continuously and the MST scale is ordinal, fine-grained ten-class prediction is often unstable in uncontrolled photographic conditions. Empirical work evaluating MST estimation under diverse lighting environments demonstrates substantial degradation in fine-grained classification performance, particularly in intermediate tone ranges \cite{Mbatha_2024_SkinToneLighting}. When tones are grouped into broader light, medium, and dark categories, estimation stability improves markedly across lighting conditions. For large-scale web-scraped imagery, where illumination and camera calibration are uncontrolled, a grouped treatment therefore provides greater robustness and reduces boundary-level annotation noise. In the context of this thesis, grouping is not a simplification of the construct but a strategy to reduce measurement variance that could otherwise obscure or artificially induce spurious correlations.

Another methodological risk arises from shortcut learning during skin tone estimation. When classifiers are trained on full images, models may exploit background, clothing, or contextual artefacts correlated with skin tone labels rather than pigmentation itself. Experimental evidence shows that full-image CNN models achieve higher raw accuracy but rely on contextual features, as revealed by Grad-CAM analyses, whereas models trained on segmented facial skin regions exhibit activations concentrated on skin pixels \cite{Matias_2024_EnhancingFairnessMST}. This distinction is critical: if annotation models rely on contextual artefacts, any subsequent correlation analysis risks detecting dataset-specific backgrounds rather than genuine pigmentation-related structure. For this reason, skin tone classification in this thesis is applied to detected facial skin regions rather than full images, reducing the likelihood that annotation labels encode spurious environmental signals.

To support the study of annotation subjectivity in skin tone labelling,
Schumann et al.~\cite{Schumann_2024_MonkSkinTone} introduced the Monk Skin
Tone Examples (MST-E) dataset, comprising 1,515 images and 31 videos of
19 subjects spanning the full ten-point MST scale. Subjects were photographed
across a range of poses, facial expressions, and lighting conditions during
a single collection session to minimise temporal variation in skin tone
appearance. Images were initially annotated by the authors and subsequently
reviewed by the creator of the MST scale, Dr.\ Ellis Monk, who provided
final annotations representing the intended MST label for each
subject. The dataset was designed primarily to support annotator training
and to investigate how consistently human raters can apply the MST scale
under varying environmental conditions.

Experimental results show that trained annotators can achieve reliable
agreement with the expert annotations even under challenging lighting
conditions, while annotators from different geographic regions exhibit
systematic variation in their MST judgments, reflecting culturally shaped
mental models of skin tone categories. Although originally intended for
annotation studies, MST-E also provides a controlled dataset that can be
used to evaluate automated skin tone estimation methods across variations
in lighting and pose, enabling analysis of performance across specific
capture conditions rather than relying solely on aggregate accuracy metrics.

%Taken together, these considerations motivate three methodological choices in this work: (i) adoption of the Monk Skin Tone scale over Fitzpatrick due to improved construct alignment and perceived inclusivity; (ii) use of grouped MST categories to enhance robustness under variable imaging conditions; and (iii) restriction of classification to segmented facial skin regions to minimise shortcut learning. Each decision is guided not by classifier accuracy alone, but by the requirement that demographic annotations function as stable and interpretable covariates in the analysis of spurious feature distributions within large-scale visual datasets.



\subsection{Limitations of Visual Demographic Inference}

A fundamental limitation of demographic annotation from visual data is the distinction between perceived attributes and self-identified attributes. Systems that infer gender from images do not measure gender identity; rather, they predict whether a subject’s appearance conforms to culturally dominant norms of gendered presentation. Scheuerman et al. demonstrate that commercial gender classification systems operationalise gender as a binary perceptual category and exhibit systematic failures on individuals whose presentation diverges from normative expectations \cite{Scheuerman_2019_HowComputersSeeGender}. In most visual datasets, gender labels are derived from annotator perception rather than self-reported identity. Consequently, what is measured is not gender identity but perceived gender expression.

This distinction has methodological implications for fairness evaluation. When generative outputs are compared to demographic statistics—such as labour force participation rates disaggregated by gender—the annotation variable and the statistical benchmark may refer to different constructs. The former reflects appearance-based perception; the latter reflects identity-based self-reporting. Any discrepancy between these constructs introduces ambiguity into downstream bias measurement.

A second limitation concerns the instability of photometric inference under varying acquisition conditions. Skin tone classifiers operate on visible colour values, which are sensitive to illumination colour temperature, exposure, sensor characteristics, and post-processing. In uncontrolled web-scale datasets such as Re-LAION-5B, images are captured under heterogeneous lighting conditions ranging from professional studio setups to low-light mobile photography. Annotation error under such variability is unlikely to be random. Certain lighting styles, aesthetic conventions, or photographic contexts may be overrepresented in particular communities or content domains, creating a confound between acquisition-level artefacts and demographic inference quality.

This issue is further compounded in synthetic imagery. In diffusion-based models such as Stable Diffusion v1.5, lighting is not an external environmental factor but a learned prior embedded in the generative model. If the model preferentially associates specific lighting distributions with certain prompts or demographic cues, automated skin tone classification applied to generated images may conflate representational bias with generative lighting bias. In this setting, annotation error can become entangled with the model’s learned visual style, making it difficult to separate genuine demographic skew from measurement artefact.

A related conceptual distinction must be drawn between race and skin tone. Race is a socially constructed category shaped by historical, political, and cultural processes; it cannot be reliably inferred from visual appearance alone. Skin tone, by contrast, is a photometric property of visible surface pigmentation. While correlated with racial categorisation in many societies, the two constructs are not equivalent. Fairness audits that operationalise race through perceived visual categorisation introduce additional layers of annotator subjectivity and cultural variability \cite{Raji_2019_ActionableAuditing}. Skin tone, though still perceptually mediated and affected by imaging conditions, is more directly grounded in observable colour properties and more closely aligned with the low-level image features examined in spurious feature analysis.

Taken together, these limitations highlight that demographic labels derived from visual inference function as measurement instruments rather than ground truth variables. They encode perceptual conventions, acquisition conditions, and annotation design choices. In large-scale visual audits, observed correlations between image features and demographic attributes may therefore reflect properties of the annotation process itself. Recognising this constraint is essential when interpreting spurious feature distributions in both web-scraped datasets and generative model outputs.


\section{Inference-Time Debiasing of Text-to-Image Diffusion 
	Models}

Approaches to mitigating demographic bias in text-to-image diffusion 
models can be broadly categorised by the stage of the model pipeline 
at which they intervene. Training-time methods address bias through 
modifications to the training data or objective function, typically 
by curating balanced datasets \cite{Yang_2020_FairerDatasets} or 
incorporating fairness-constrained losses during model training 
\cite{Zhang_2018_MitigatingUnwantedBiasesAdversarial}. Fine-tuning methods adjust 
pre-trained model weights on carefully constructed demographic 
reference corpora \cite{Friedrich_2023_FairDiffusionInstructingTextToImage}, inheriting 
the backbone's expressive capacity while redirecting its prior. Both 
families of approaches can achieve thorough debiasing but require 
substantial computational resources and high-quality annotated 
reference data, placing them beyond reach for practitioners working 
with pre-trained deployed systems \cite{Jiang_2025_FairGen}. 
Furthermore, training-time interventions applied to models whose 
weights have not been released cannot be independently reproduced 
or verified by the research community.

Inference-time methods represent a third category that operates on 
a frozen model during the generation process itself, requiring no 
retraining and no access to model weights beyond those already 
publicly available. This family of methods is particularly relevant 
for widely deployed open-source systems such as Stable Diffusion 
v1.5, where the frozen model is a fixed artefact and the only 
available intervention points are the conditioning signal, the 
sampling trajectory, and the model's internal attention mechanisms. 
The methods surveyed in this section address demographic bias through 
three distinct mechanisms: editing the internal representations of 
the model via closed-form weight modifications, manipulating the 
text conditioning signal through learned inclusive embeddings, and 
guiding the denoising trajectory through external signals targeting 
spatial structure or colour distribution.

\subsubsection*{Weight Editing: Unified Concept Editing}

Unified Concept Editing (UCE) \cite{Gandikota_2024_UCE} addresses 
bias by directly editing the cross-attention projections of a 
diffusion model through a closed-form solution, without any 
gradient-based training. The method is grounded in the observation 
that the value projection matrices $W_v$ of the U-Net's 
cross-attention layers encode associations between text token 
embeddings and the visual features attended to during denoising. 
By modifying these projections, one can alter the model's behaviour 
for specific concepts — such as the visual prior associated with 
occupational prompts — without affecting unrelated generation 
behaviour.

Formally, UCE frames debiasing as a constrained least-squares 
problem over $W_v$ at each attention layer. Given a set of target 
concept embeddings representing the biased associations to be 
corrected and a set of anchor concept embeddings representing 
unrelated content to be preserved, UCE computes a closed-form 
update $\Delta W_v$ that minimises the change to anchor concept 
representations while steering the target concept representations 
toward a more balanced demographic prior. This closed-form solution 
is derived from a linearisation of the attention layer's behaviour 
around the target token embeddings, requiring no forward passes 
through the denoising network beyond computing a small number of 
text embeddings. Because the edit is applied layer-wise and 
accumulated across attention layers, each layer contributes 
independently to the overall concept redirection, and the 
closed-form solution at each layer does not account for the 
downstream effects of edits applied to other layers.

A key strength of UCE is its scalability to concurrent edits across 
multiple concepts. The authors demonstrate simultaneous debiasing 
across up to 35 occupational prompts alongside style erasure and 
content moderation within a single edited model, with the 
constrained optimisation preventing individual edits from 
accumulating into mutual interference. This multi-concept 
composability is a significant practical advantage over methods 
that address one bias axis at a time.

However, UCE has notable limitations. Because the edit operates on 
attention projection matrices globally, it applies uniformly across 
all spatial positions and all prompts that activate the targeted 
concept representation — the method cannot selectively debias 
outputs in specific image regions or under specific contextual 
conditions. The linear approximation underlying the closed-form 
solution furthermore constrains the method's ability to correct 
strongly non-linear biases embedded simultaneously across multiple 
layers. Finally, UCE provides no mechanism for specifying a precise 
target demographic distribution: it steers representations away from 
a dominant prior but cannot guarantee convergence to a specified 
demographic breakdown.

\subsubsection*{Inclusive Prompt Embeddings: ITI-GEN}

A fundamentally different approach to demographic control operates 
at the level of the text conditioning signal rather than the model 
weights. ITI-GEN \cite{Zhang_2023_ITIGEN}, presented as an oral 
and best paper finalist at ICCV 2023, begins from the observation 
that demographic attributes such as skin tone are difficult to 
express unambiguously through natural language. Textual descriptions 
of demographic categories are inherently imprecise: the word 
``darker-skinned'' does not uniquely specify a point in the model's 
prior over skin tones, and the linguistic ambiguity encoded in 
CLIP's text representations of such terms further limits the 
controllability of direct prompt engineering. ITI-GEN resolves this 
by replacing linguistic specification with image-grounded 
specification, leveraging the principle that reference images can 
represent demographic concepts more precisely than text.

The method learns a set of \textit{inclusive tokens}: short 
learnable embedding vectors inserted into the CLIP text encoder's 
input space that are optimised to produce generated images uniformly 
distributed across the categories of a target demographic attribute. 
For each attribute category, a set of reference images is selected 
that exemplifies the category, and the corresponding token embedding 
is trained via two losses through backpropagation through the frozen 
diffusion model. A \textit{direction alignment loss} 
$\mathcal{L}^m_\text{dir}$ encourages the learned token to move 
the text embedding in the direction of the CLIP image embedding of 
the reference images for that category, establishing a correspondence 
between the token and the target attribute. A \textit{semantic 
	consistency loss} $\mathcal{L}^m_\text{sem}$ penalises divergence 
from the original prompt's semantic content, ensuring that the 
inclusive token supplements rather than overwrites the user's 
intended generation context. Together these losses produce token 
embeddings that, when prepended to a generation prompt at inference 
time, steer the model toward the target demographic category while 
preserving semantic fidelity to the prompt. It should be noted that 
this optimisation objective provides only approximate balancing 
toward a uniform distribution rather than a guaranteed uniform 
outcome, as the correspondence between learned embeddings and 
generated attribute distributions depends on the expressiveness 
of the CLIP token space.

A crucial methodological advantage is that training requires only 
datasets capturing the \textit{marginal} distribution of each 
attribute individually, bypassing the combinatorial difficulty of 
assembling datasets representing joint distributions across multiple 
attributes simultaneously. For $A$ attributes each with $K$ 
categories, one requires $A \times K$ reference image sets rather 
than $K^A$ joint combinations. The resulting tokens are composable 
at inference time by concatenation, enabling simultaneous control 
over multiple attributes. Importantly, ITI-GEN does not modify 
model weights and is directly compatible with other conditioning 
frameworks including ControlNet \cite{LZhang_2023_ControlNet} and 
InstructPix2Pix, making it naturally composable with structural and 
stylistic conditioning signals.

A reproducibility study by Gallo Fern\'andez et al.\ 
\cite{GalloFernandez_2024_ITIGENRepro} identifies two important 
limitations. First, ITI-GEN is susceptible to learning spurious 
proxy features from its reference images: if the reference images 
for a demographic category are systematically correlated with 
background, clothing, or lighting properties, the learned token may 
encode these spurious cues rather than the target attribute itself, 
precisely mirroring the shortcut learning pathology identified in 
discriminative models. Second, the exponential growth of the joint 
attribute combination space with increasing numbers of attributes 
makes inclusive generation for all joint combinations increasingly 
difficult, as the model struggles to simultaneously satisfy all 
demographic constraints when their number grows large.

\subsubsection*{Self-Discovering Latent Directions: FairGen}

FairGen \cite{Jiang_2025_FairGen} addresses a structural limitation 
shared by both UCE and ITI-GEN: their dependence on externally 
curated reference datasets or annotation models to define the target 
demographic directions. UCE requires specification of which concept 
representations to steer and toward what target; ITI-GEN requires 
reference image sets per category. Both are therefore constrained 
by the quality and coverage of these external resources, and both 
introduce annotation cost that scales with the number of target 
attributes. FairGen instead proposes to discover the demographic 
latent directions directly from the model itself through a 
self-supervised training procedure, eliminating external annotation 
requirements.

The method attaches a set of small, lightweight adapters to the 
cross-attention layers of the frozen U-Net, one adapter per target 
attribute. Each adapter is optimised to learn the direction in the 
model's latent feature space along which the target attribute varies, 
by manipulating the noise prediction function through a noise 
composition objective that exploits the structure of the DDPM 
training process. This allows the latent direction for a given 
demographic attribute to be extracted without requiring any 
labelled demographic data. Orthogonal regularisation between 
adapters for different attributes reduces interference when 
debiasing multiple attributes simultaneously. Without this 
regularisation, adapters trained on correlated attributes — such as 
gender and age, which co-vary in training data — tend to encode 
overlapping directions that interfere destructively when activated 
together, and the ablation study in the original paper demonstrates 
substantial degradation in both fairness and image quality when it 
is removed \cite{Jiang_2025_FairGen}.

At inference time, the adapter corresponding to a target demographic 
category is activated with a weight determined by a distribution 
indicator, which tracks the demographic composition of images 
generated so far in a batch and steers subsequent generation toward 
the prescribed target distribution. This mechanism enforces fairness 
globally across a batch rather than independently per image: rather 
than randomising each individual output, it maintains running 
statistics and adjusts the conditioning of future samples to correct 
any accumulated distributional imbalance. Adapter activation 
introduces negligible computational overhead at inference time, 
meaning FairGen matches the inference speed of the unmodified Stable 
Diffusion model. The primary limitation relative to ITI-GEN is that 
the discovered latent directions are model-specific and 
non-transferable: the self-discovering training process must be 
repeated for each base model architecture, whereas ITI-GEN's learned 
tokens have been shown to transfer across different model 
architectures.

\subsubsection*{Spatial Conditioning: ControlNet}

The three methods described above target the demographic content of 
generated images but leave the spatial composition of the generation 
unconstrained. ControlNet \cite{LZhang_2023_ControlNet} addresses 
the orthogonal problem of adding fine-grained spatial conditioning 
to a pre-trained diffusion model, enabling the structure of generated 
images to be constrained by auxiliary signals such as pose skeletons, 
depth maps, Canny edge maps, or semantic segmentation masks.

The central architectural challenge is to introduce spatial 
conditioning without destabilising the large pre-trained backbone. 
ControlNet resolves this through a trainable copy of the frozen 
U-Net encoder and middle block. This copy receives the additional 
spatial conditioning signal alongside the standard inputs and 
processes it through the same encoder structure as the original 
model, benefiting from the pre-trained feature representations. The 
outputs of the trainable copy are injected back into the frozen 
backbone via residual connections at each encoder level and at the 
middle block, allowing the backbone's decoder to integrate spatial 
conditioning information during the upsampling pass where image 
structure is synthesised. Multiple ControlNet conditioning signals 
can furthermore be composed at inference time by summing their 
residual contributions, enabling simultaneous constraint of multiple 
spatial properties without retraining.

To prevent the randomly initialised conditioning branch from 
corrupting the frozen backbone's learned representations during 
early training, all residual connections from the trainable copy 
are implemented as zero-initialised convolution layers 
(\textit{zero convolutions}). At training onset, these layers 
produce zero output, meaning the backbone behaves identically to 
the unmodified model and no harmful noise from the random 
initialisation of the trainable copy reaches the frozen weights. 
As training proceeds, the zero convolution weights grow from zero 
in proportion to the gradient signal, ensuring that conditioning 
information is introduced progressively and only as the trainable 
copy learns meaningful representations. This design permits 
ControlNet training to be robust even on datasets of fewer than 
50,000 images, a significant practical advantage given that 
collecting annotated conditioning data at scale is expensive.

In the context of debiasing, ControlNet is relevant because many 
demographic spurious features operate at the structural level 
rather than through colour or semantic content alone. As documented 
by Meister et al.\ \cite{Meister_2023_GenderArtifactsinVisualDatasets}, body pose 
distributions, person-to-frame scale, and spatial framing patterns 
constitute significant gender artifacts in visual datasets that are 
not removed by purely semantic or colouristic debiasing 
interventions. By providing an explicit structural conditioning 
signal — for example, a neutral canonical pose skeleton — ControlNet 
can constrain the spatial properties of generated images 
independently of the semantic and demographic properties governed 
by the text prompt and inclusive token embeddings, enabling both 
forms of control to be composed without mutual interference.

\subsubsection*{Colour Distribution Guidance: SW-Guidance and 
	Chamfer-Based Alignment}

A further class of inference-time methods targets the colour 
statistics of generated images directly, motivated by the observation 
that text-to-image models trained on aesthetically filtered 
web-scraped corpora learn strong colour priors that may correlate 
with demographic attributes. Consistent with the spurious feature 
analysis of Meister et al.\ \cite{Meister_2023_GenderArtifactsinVisualDatasets}, 
who demonstrate that mean RGB channel values are sufficient to 
discriminate gender-labelled images above chance, colour 
distributions in training data can function as low-level proxies 
for demographic group membership. A model that has absorbed these 
colour-demographic correlations will tend to reproduce them at 
generation time, producing colour palettes that are systematically 
skewed across demographic categories even when the conditioning 
prompt contains no colour-specifying language.

SW-Guidance \cite{Lobashev_2025_SWGuidance} is a training-free 
method that introduces colour conditioning into the diffusion 
sampling process by modifying the noise prediction at each 
denoising step. Concretely, at each step the gradient of the Sliced 
1-Wasserstein (SW) distance between the colour distribution of the 
current partially denoised image and a reference colour palette is 
computed and added to the denoising network's noise prediction 
$\epsilon_\theta(x_t, t)$, functioning as an additional guidance 
term analogous to classifier-free guidance \cite{Ho_2022_CFG}. A 
guidance scale hyperparameter controls the strength of the colour 
correction relative to the text-conditioned score, with higher 
values producing stronger colour adherence at the risk of 
destabilising the semantic content of the generation — a trade-off 
analogous to the prompt adherence versus diversity trade-off 
introduced by increasing the CFG scale $s$ in standard sampling. 
The update nudges the denoising trajectory toward the region of the 
data manifold consistent with the target colour distribution without 
explicitly constraining the semantic content of the generated image.

The Sliced Wasserstein distance is chosen specifically because it 
is efficiently differentiable. Full optimal transport between 
high-dimensional colour distributions is computationally 
intractable, but the Sliced Wasserstein distance approximates it 
by projecting the two distributions onto a large number of random 
one-dimensional slices and computing the one-dimensional Wasserstein 
distance on each slice — a problem with a closed-form solution via 
sorting. SW-Guidance supports multiple Wasserstein-based loss 
variants including standard Sliced Wasserstein (SWD), 
Distributional Sliced Wasserstein (DSWD), Generalised Sliced 
Wasserstein (GSWD), and Importance-Sampled Energy-Based Sliced 
Wasserstein (ISEBSW), each trading off approximation quality 
against computational cost. A notable practical limitation is that 
computing the guidance gradient requires backpropagation through 
the entire denoising U-Net at every timestep, introducing 
substantial computational overhead relative to standard sampling 
and making SW-Guidance impractical for large-scale image generation.

Shum et al.\ \cite{Shum_2025_ColorAlignment} propose an alternative 
colour alignment approach that is substantially faster because it 
does not require backpropagation through the denoising network. 
Their method operates by projecting diffusion terms — either the 
partially denoised latent representation or the predicted clean 
image $\hat{x}_0$ — directly into a conditional colour manifold 
defined by a reference palette, using Chamfer distance to establish 
correspondences between the colour point clouds of the generated 
and reference images. The Chamfer distance between two point clouds 
$X$ and $Y$ is defined as:
\begin{equation}
	d_\text{Chamfer}(X, Y) = \sum_{x \in X} \min_{y \in Y} 
	\|x - y\|_2^2 + \sum_{y \in Y} \min_{x \in X} \|x - y\|_2^2
\end{equation}
and measures the total nearest-neighbour discrepancy between the 
two point sets, providing a differentiable measure of how well the 
generated colour distribution covers the reference palette and 
vice versa.

At each denoising step where colour alignment is active, an 
iterative matching procedure is applied: the Chamfer-optimal 
correspondence between the generated colour point cloud and the 
reference is computed, and a gradient step is taken with respect 
to this distance to project the predicted $\hat{x}_0$ toward the 
reference distribution. This projected $\hat{x}_0$ is then used 
in place of the original predicted clean image in the standard DDPM 
recomposition formula to produce the next denoising step. 
Importantly, alignment is applied only during early denoising 
timesteps above a configurable threshold, reflecting the empirical 
observation that global colour structure is established early in 
the denoising trajectory while fine structural and semantic details 
emerge during later steps. Below this threshold, alignment is 
explicitly disabled so that the iterative projection does not 
corrupt the fine-grained content forming during the late denoising 
phase. This placement captures the stage of the trajectory at which 
colour corrections have the most leverage over the final image 
while preserving the semantic fidelity of the output.


\section{Occupations as a Probe for Demographic Bias in Text-to-Image Generation}
\label{subsec:occupations}

Occupations represent one of the most well-validated and widely adopted analytical lenses for
studying demographic bias in AI systems. Job titles carry historically accumulated
societal associations between professional roles and demographic attributes which are extensively documented in real-world labour statistics and deeply reflected in the
large-scale text and image corpora on which modern models are trained hence, neutral occupation
prompts provide a transparent and interpretable window onto a model's learnt demographic
priors.  This property has made occupations a standard experimental instrument across both
natural language processing and, more recently, text-to-image generation research.  The
present thesis adopts occupation-based prompts as its primary analytical axis, drawing on this
body of work to motivate and validate the choice.

\subsection{Occupational Stereotyping in Language and Vision Systems}
\label{subsubsec:occupational_stereo}

Evidence that AI systems acquire and reproduce occupational stereotypes is well established
across a range of model families.  In natural language processing, Nadeem et al.\
\cite{Nadeem_2020_StereoSet} demonstrate through their large-scale \textsc{StereoSet}
benchmark covering 120 profession-related target terms spanning roles from
\emph{software developer} to \emph{housekeeper}, that all major pretrained language models,
including BERT, GPT-2, RoBERTa, and XLNet, exhibit significant stereotypical preferences when
presented with profession-related contexts.  Notably, they find a consistent positive
relationship between a model's language modelling ability and the strength of its
stereotypical biases, suggesting that stereotype acquisition is not an incidental failure mode
but a systematic consequence of learning from real-world text distributions.  This finding is
directly relevant to the present work because the text encoders such as CLIP that condition text-to-image
models such as Stable Diffusion are pretrained on precisely such
distributions, providing a theoretically grounded pathway through which occupational
stereotypes encoded at the language level propagate into generated imagery.

In the vision-language domain, Harrison et al.~\cite{Harrison_2023_RunLikeAGirl} demonstrate
a related phenomenon at the level of image captioning data.  Analysing the ManyNames dataset,
they find that human annotators systematically apply role-based names (e.g.\ \emph{surfer},
\emph{tennis player}) more frequently to male athletes than to female athletes performing
identical activities, with an average of 46\% sport-related names for male subjects versus
35\% for female subjects.  Because captioning datasets of this kind form part of the training
corpora for large vision-language models, such annotation asymmetries constitute a direct
mechanism through which occupational and role-based concepts become demographically skewed in
model representations.  This work reinforces the motivation for using occupation-based prompts
as a diagnostic tool: if the training data systematically under-represents certain groups in
role-related language, occupation prompts are precisely the context in which those imbalances
will manifest most visibly.

\subsection{Occupational Bias in Text-to-Image Models}
\label{subsubsec:naik_occupations}

Within the specific domain of text-to-image generation, Naik and Nushi\
\cite{Naik_2023_SocialBiasesThroughTheTextToImageGenerationLens} provide a comprehensive audit confirming that
occupation prompts reliably surface severe representational biases in both DALL-E~v2 and
Stable Diffusion v1.  Generating images using neutral prompts such as \emph{a photo of a
	doctor} or \emph{a photo of a nurse} and annotating the outputs for perceived gender, race, and age, they find that model
outputs in many cases deviate dramatically from real-world occupational demographics as
reported by the US Bureau of Labor Statistics.  Occupations such as \emph{chief executive
	officer} and \emph{computer programmer} yield images that are almost exclusively male and
white, while occupations such as \emph{nurse} and \emph{housekeeper} yield images that are
almost exclusively female.  Chuang et al.~\cite{Chuang_2023_DebiasingVisionLanguageModelsViaBiasedPrompts} further
demonstrate that these biases are not surface-level prompt-sensitivity artefacts but are
embedded in the structure of the CLIP text embedding space itself, with demographic
directions geometrically separable from occupational content.  Together, this body of
evidence confirms that occupational prompts are not merely one possible experimental design
choice among many but a particularly reliable and interpretable probe for the kinds of
demographic bias that arise when models are trained on web-scale, uncurated data.

\subsection{Occupation Classification: The ISCO-08 Framework}
\label{subsubsec:isco}

For occupation-based probing to yield generalisable and unbiased results, the prompt set must
be drawn from a principled taxonomy that covers the full breadth of occupational space rather
than over-representing stereotypically gendered or racialised roles selected by convenience.
The International Labour Organization's \emph{International Standard Classification of
	Occupations} (ISCO-08)~\cite{ILO_ISCO08} provides this foundation.  ISCO-08 organises the
universe of occupations into a four-level hierarchy: ten major groups (e.g.\
\emph{Professionals}, \emph{Service and Sales Workers}, \emph{Craft and Related Trades
	Workers}), 43 sub-major groups, 130 minor groups, and 436 unit groups, each assigned a
numeric code and a skill-level designation ranging from elementary occupations at level~1
through highly qualified professionals at level~4.  The classification defines an
\emph{occupation} as ``a set of jobs whose main tasks and duties are characterised by a high
degree of similarity'', grouping roles jointly by \emph{skill level} and \emph{skill
	specialisation}~\cite{ILO_ISCO08}.

In this thesis, ISCO-08 serves as the organising framework for the construction of the
occupation prompt set.  By sampling job titles systematically across all ten major groups, the
resulting prompt set spans a representative range of social prestige, typical skill
requirements, and cultural connotation.  This ensures that any bias measurements obtained are
reflective of the model's general behaviour across occupational space rather than being driven
by a small subset of roles that happen to carry strong stereotypical associations.  The use of
an internationally standardised classification also facilitates comparison with prior work that
has likewise adopted ISCO-08 or related occupational taxonomies as a reference
point~\cite{Naik_2023_SocialBiasesThroughTheTextToImageGenerationLens}.

Low-confidence predictions may result from genuine perceptual ambiguity in the generated face, or from generative artefacts that reduce the quality of the classifier's internal representation. Without validation against human annotations on a held-out set of synthetic images, it is not possible to tell which of these applies. In this thesis, confidence thresholds are used as a principled way to select a high-quality annotation subset, while acknowledging that the retained set may not represent the full distribution of generated images. This limitation is carried through to the interpretation of demographic correlation results. How to validate demographic annotation tools on synthetic image distributions remains an open problem, and no established standard practice existed at the time of writing.



%A fundamental yet underexplored dimension of this problem involves spurious features—low-level, non-semantic image characteristics including color statistics, texture patterns, edge distributions, frequency domain properties, and compression artifacts [11, 12]. Unlike semantic features that capture meaningful visual content (e.g., objects, scenes, actions), spurious features represent superficial statistical regularities that arise from data collection processes, image sources, camera characteristics, or post-processing pipelines.

%First, different image sources exhibit distinct spurious signatures—a phenomenon sometimes termed "dataset fingerprinting" or "dataset personality" [15, 16]. Web-scraped datasets, social media images, professional photography, and synthetic images each carry characteristic statistical patterns unrelated to their semantic content. When classifiers can reliably distinguish between image sources using only these low-level features, it indicates that spurious patterns are systematically embedded in data distributions.
%Second, and most critically for fairness, spurious features may correlate with sensitive attributes such as gender, ethnicity, or age. If certain demographic groups are disproportionately represented in images with specific color palettes, lighting conditions, image quality levels, or compression artifacts within the training data, models can learn to exploit these superficial cues for demographic classification. This creates a particularly insidious form of bias: predictions ostensibly based on semantic content may actually rely on non-semantic shortcuts invisible to human observers and standard evaluation metrics.
%Third, the generative training process itself may transform spurious feature distributions in complex ways. It remains unclear whether diffusion models faithfully reproduce the spurious patterns present in training data, systematically amplify certain features while suppressing others, or introduce entirely new spurious correlations through the learned denoising process. Understanding this transformation is essential for predicting how training data characteristics will manifest in deployed systems.
