A key challenge in deploying machine learning models reliably is their tendency to learn
features that are predictive within the training data but carry no causal or semantic
relationship to the target variable. Such features are termed \emph{spurious features}, and
the statistical associations they form with target labels are known as \emph{spurious correlations}~\cite{Arjovsky_2020_InvariantRiskMinimization,Sagawa_2020_DistributionallyRobustNeuralNetworksForGroupShifts}. Formally, a spurious correlation exists when a feature $f$ co-occurs with a label $y$ across the training distribution $p_{\text{train}}(f, y)$ but this co-occurrence does not persist under distribution shift, i.e., $p_{\text{train}}(f \mid y) \neq p_{\text{test}}(f \mid y)$, or
when $f$ carries no causal information about $y$ despite being statistically correlated with
it. A canonical illustration is a gender classifier trained on a dataset where women are
disproportionately photographed wearing dresses: the model may learn clothing type as a
reliable decision cue rather than facial or morphological features. This correlation holds
within the training distribution but breaks under shift, for instance when the model
encounters images of men in kilts or women in suits, exposing the feature as spurious. A
model that learns to exploit such a feature in lieu of causally relevant ones is said to
learn a \emph{shortcut}~\cite{Geirhos_2020_ShortcutLearningInDeepNeuralNetworks}.

Geirhos et al.~\cite{Geirhos_2020_ShortcutLearningInDeepNeuralNetworks} provide a
comprehensive treatment of shortcut learning in the deep learning literature. They
characterise a shortcut as any decision rule that performs well on the training distribution
by exploiting a feature correlated with the correct label but which does not reflect the
underlying task concept. Shortcuts are distinguished from genuinely useful features not by
their statistical properties within the training set but by their failure to generalise when
the spurious correlation is broken, either through natural distribution shift or deliberate
intervention. Critically, Geirhos et al.\ argue that shortcut learning is not a pathological
edge case but a rational consequence of empirical risk minimisation: given a choice between a
reliable spurious cue and a harder-to-learn causal feature, gradient-based optimisation will
consistently favour whichever feature reduces training loss most efficiently.

\subsection*{Taxonomies of Spurious Features in Computer Vision}

Spurious features in visual data can be broadly categorised along two dimensions: the level
of the image hierarchy at which they reside, and whether they reflect properties of the
subject, the context, or the acquisition process.

At the \emph{pixel and statistical level}, low-level image attributes encompassing mean colour channel values, brightness distribution, contrast statistics, JPEG compression artefacts among others can serve as reliable proxies for class labels, thereby enabling shortcut learning particularly when the dataset collection process introduces systematic correlations between acquisition conditions and class membership. Jo and Bengio  in \cite{Jo_2017_SurfaceStatisticalRegularities} demonstrate that Convolutional Neural Networks (\acrshort{cnn}) trained on benchmark datasets are inclined to learn surface statistical regularities as opposed to high level abstractions, whilst achieving high classification accuracy even when these statistical regularities bear no semantic relationship to the class concept. Furthermore, Geirhos et al. in \cite{Geirhos_2019_TextureBias} demonstrates how ImageNet-trained CNNs exhibit a strong \emph{texture bias} opting to rely on texture for object classification as opposed to shape, particularly apparent when the texture of one object is rendered onto the shape of another.

At the \emph{frequency domain level}, the Fourier decomposition of images separates
low-frequency components (smooth colour gradients, global illumination) from high-frequency
components (edges, fine textures, compression ringing).  Wang et al. \cite{Wang_2020_HighFrequencyComponentHelpsExplainTheGeneralizationOfCNN}
demonstrate that CNNs can exploit high-frequency features that are imperceptible to the human
eye as spurious shortcuts, and that adversarially introduced high-frequency perturbations can
drastically alter model decisions without any perceptible change to image content.  This
sensitivity is particularly concerning in datasets assembled from diverse web sources,
where different image origins often carry systematically different frequency signatures
arising from camera hardware, processing pipelines, and compression settings.

At the \emph{contextual and background level}, spurious features can arise from statistical
regularities in scene composition rather than in the subject itself.  Beery et
al. \cite{Beery_2018_RecognitionTerraIncognita} demonstrate this dramatically in a wildlife
recognition setting, showing that classifiers trained on images where animals consistently
appear against certain background types fail severely when the same animal appears against
an unfamiliar background, revealing that the background has been learned as a more reliable
shortcut than animal morphology.  Xiao et al. \cite{Xiao_2021_NoiseOrSignal} reach similar
conclusions in the context of ImageNet, demonstrating that models achieve non-trivial
classification accuracy when trained and tested on background-only images with the foreground
objects masked, establishing that contextual scene statistics constitute a pervasive spurious
signal in standard benchmarks.  Torralba and Efros \cite{Torralba_2011_UnbiasedLookDatasetBias}
earlier identified a related phenomenon at the dataset level: models trained on one visual
dataset and evaluated on another exhibit sharp performance drops, demonstrating that much of
what models learn reflects dataset-specific statistical regularities rather than
generalisable visual concepts.

At the \emph{semantic and co-occurrence level}, spurious correlations can be encoded in
higher-level statistical regularities such as the tendency for certain object categories to
co-occur with certain scenes or for certain demographic attributes to be associated with
particular activities.  These correlations are not artefacts of image acquisition but reflect
genuine societal patterns or collection biases that are absorbed into the training distribution
and subsequently exploited by the model~\cite{Zhao_2017_MenAlsoLikeShopping}.

\subsection*{Understanding Bias in Large-Scale Visual Datasets}
\label{subsec:zeng2024}
% -----------------------------------------------------------------

Zeng et al.~\cite{Zeng_2024_UnderstandingBiasinLargeScaleVisualDatasets} address a
complementary but distinct problem: characterising the concrete forms of bias that cause
large-scale pre-training datasets to be trivially distinguishable by modern neural networks.
Their starting point is the dataset classification task, originally introduced by Torralba and
Efros~\cite{Torralba_2011_UnbiasedLookDatasetBias} in 2011 and revisited by Liu and
He~\cite{Liu_2024_DecadeBattleDatasetBias} in 2024, who showed that even the largest and
most diverse modern datasets --- including YFCC100M~\cite{Thomee_2016_YFCC100M},
CC12M~\cite{Changpinyo_2021_CC12M}, and DataComp-1B~\cite{Gadre_2023_DataComp} --- can be
classified with high accuracy by a ConvNeXt model, achieving 84.7\,\% accuracy across the
three-class task.  While this finding establishes that the datasets are biased, it does not
identify \emph{which} properties of the images are responsible.

\subsubsection*{Framework and Datasets}

Zeng et al.\ address this explanatory gap by proposing a transformation-based framework.  The
core idea is simple and powerful: apply an image transformation that selectively preserves
one type of visual information while suppressing others, train a dataset classifier on the
transformed images, and use the resulting accuracy as a proxy for how much bias resides in
that specific type of information.  The three datasets studied --- YFCC100M, CC12M, and
DataComp-1B, collectively denoted YCD --- are representative of the large-scale
internet-scraped corpora used to pre-train modern vision and vision-language models.  For
each transformation, 1M and 10K images are sampled per dataset for training and validation
respectively, and a ConvNeXt-Tiny model is trained for 30 epochs.  A reference accuracy of
82.0\,\% on unmodified images establishes the performance ceiling against which transformed
results are interpreted.

\subsubsection*{Semantic Transformations}

The first family of transformations targets semantic content.  Semantic segmentation using
ViT-Adapter-Large on the ADE20K vocabulary (150 classes) converts each image into a
colour-coded class map, discarding texture while retaining spatial object layout; the
resulting classifier achieves 69.8\,\% accuracy.  Object detection with ViTDet-Huge on the
LVIS vocabulary (1,203 classes) retains bounding boxes with class labels but discards internal
pixel information; accuracy falls to 61.9\,\%.  Image captioning with LLaVA~1.5 discards
all visual information in favour of text, yet short captions still support 63.8\,\% accuracy
and long paragraph captions 66.1\,\%, performances close to that of semantic segmentation.
Together these results establish that \emph{semantic bias} --- differences in which categories
of objects appear in each dataset --- is a major driver of overall dataset distinguishability.

A variational autoencoder (VAE) experiment complements this finding.  Reconstructing images
through the bottleneck of a pretrained Stable Diffusion VAE reduces accuracy only modestly,
from 82.0\,\% to 77.4\,\%, indicating that low-level signatures such as JPEG compression
artefacts or resolution inconsistencies contribute relatively little compared to semantic
content.

\subsubsection*{Structural Transformations}

The second family of transformations isolates spatial geometry and object shape while
discarding semantic labels.  Canny edge detection yields 71.0\,\% accuracy; SAM-based
class-agnostic contour extraction achieves 73.2\,\%; and monocular depth estimation with
Depth-Anything-V2 yields 73.1\,\%.  Notably, these structure-only representations
outperform most of the semantic transformations, demonstrating that differences in the
\emph{shapes and spatial arrangements of objects} are at least as informative as differences
in object category for distinguishing dataset origin.

\subsubsection*{Spatial Permutation Experiments}

To disentangle local structure from global spatial relationships, Zeng et al.\ conduct
pixel-level and patch-level shuffling experiments.  Randomly shuffling individual pixels
destroys all spatial coherence and forces the classifier to rely solely on the colour
distribution of pixels; accuracy drops sharply to 52.2\,\%, only marginally above the 33.3\,\%
chance level.  Shuffling patches of size $16\!\times\!16$, however, recovers nearly all
discriminability, with accuracy reaching 81.2\,\%.  This result demonstrates that
\emph{local} spatial structure --- the arrangement of pixels within small neighbourhoods ---
is sufficient to identify dataset origin, and that global image composition is far less
important than local texture and shape patterns.

\subsubsection*{Colour Statistics}

Building on the pixel-shuffling result, a mean-RGB experiment compresses each image to a
single constant colour representing the per-channel average.  Even this extreme abstraction
achieves 48.5\,\% accuracy, roughly 15 percentage points above chance.  Inspection of the
per-channel distributions reveals that YFCC images are substantially darker on average than
CC or DataComp images, a difference that likely reflects YFCC's strong representation of
outdoor photography under varied lighting conditions.

\subsubsection*{Frequency Domain Analysis}

Applying ideal high-pass and low-pass filters in the frequency domain shows that dataset-level
bias exists across the full frequency spectrum: high-frequency components (texture, sharp
edges) yield 79.2\,\% accuracy while low-frequency components (smooth colour gradients,
global structure) yield 70.4\,\%, both close to the reference accuracy.  This finding has
direct implications for spurious feature analysis: a model trained on a biased dataset will
encounter frequency-domain shortcuts at multiple scales simultaneously, making mitigation
strategies that target only one frequency regime insufficient.

\subsubsection*{Synthetic Image Classification}

Zeng et al.\ also investigate whether dataset bias propagates into images generated by
diffusion models trained on each dataset.  Unconditional diffusion models (DiT) trained
separately on YFCC, CC, and DataComp produce synthetic images that can be classified with
77.6\,\% accuracy, nearly matching the reference, demonstrating that \emph{generative models
	inherit and preserve the biases present in their training data}.  A weaker signal (58.1\,\%)
is obtained when a single pretrained SDXL-Turbo model is used to generate images conditioned
on LLaVA captions, confirming that the semantic content of captions partly transmits dataset
identity even across generative models.

\subsubsection*{Semantic Explanation via Object-Level and Language Analysis}

Beyond classifier accuracy, Zeng et al.\ provide qualitative explanations of the semantic
differences using two complementary approaches.  Object-level analysis with logistic
regression on binary object-presence vectors (from ImageNet, LVIS, and ADE20K vocabularies)
shows that YFCC images are dominated by outdoor infrastructure (traffic lights, poles,
stages), while CC and DataComp images are dominated by household items, products, and digital
graphics.  YFCC also exhibits substantially higher per-image object diversity than CC or
DataComp, suggesting that its user-generated photography context captures richer, less
curated scenes.  Open-ended language analysis using LDA topic modelling and GPT-4o
summarisation of LLaVA-generated captions consistently characterises YFCC as depicting
outdoor and human-activity-centred content, DataComp as featuring static product imagery on
clean backgrounds, and CC as a blend of the two.



\subsection{Gender Artifacts in Visual Datasets}
\label{subsec:meister2023}
% -----------------------------------------------------------------

Meister et al.~\cite{Meister_2023_GenderArtifactsinVisualDatasets} present a systematic
investigation into the nature and prevalence of \emph{gender artifacts} in large-scale
computer vision datasets.  The authors define a gender artifact as a visual cue that is both
\emph{learnable} --- in the sense that a modern image classifier can exploit it to distinguish
between images labelled female and images labelled male --- and \emph{interpretable}, meaning
it has a recognisable human corollary such as colour, shape, or scene composition.  This dual
constraint deliberately scopes the analysis to the most practically consequential
correlations: those that automated systems can learn and that carry socially legible meaning.

The work is motivated by a critical gap in the existing fairness literature.  A prevailing
family of bias-mitigation techniques, collectively termed \emph{fairness through blindness},
operates under the assumption that removing a person's visible appearance from an image is sufficient to eliminate the gendered signal.  Meister et al.\ challenge this assumption
directly by asking a more fundamental question: to what extent can gendered information truly
be excised from a visual dataset?

An important methodological note shapes how the results should be interpreted.  The authors
deliberately refer to their model as a \emph{gender artifact model} rather than a gender
classifier to emphasise that it is a diagnostic tool for understanding dataset correlations,
not a system intended to recognise gender identity.  They further acknowledge that their
binarised operationalisation of gender expression --- derived from external annotators'
perceptions rather than self-reported identity --- reflects the limitations of existing
dataset annotation practices and is used solely to expose distributional differences in the
data.

\subsubsection*{Datasets and Model}

The analysis centres on two widely used object-recognition benchmarks: Microsoft
COCO~\cite{Lin_2014_COCO} and OpenImages~\cite{Kuznetsova_2020_OpenImagesV4}.  Gender
labels are derived from existing annotations rather than manual re-annotation; for COCO,
labels are inferred from image captions using a keyword-matching heuristic following prior
work~\cite{Zhao_2017_MenAlsoLikeShopping}, while OpenImages labels are drawn from the More
Inclusive Annotations for People (MIAP) subset~\cite{Schumann_2021_StepInclusivePeopleAnnotations}.
Both datasets are noted to be skewed male, with 69.2\,\% and 62.9\,\% male-labelled images
in COCO and OpenImages respectively.  The gender artifact model is a
ResNet-50~\cite{He_2016_DeepResidualLearning} pretrained on ImageNet, whose hyperparameters
are tuned on a held-out validation set.  Performance is reported as the area under the ROC
curve (AUC), where 50\,\% represents chance-level discrimination.

\subsubsection*{Resolution and Colour Artifacts}

The first experimental series investigates artifacts residing in the global perceptual
properties of images.  Images are progressively downsampled from $224\!\times\!224$ to
$7\!\times\!7$ pixels, with the gender artifact model trained and evaluated separately at
each resolution.  In colour images, the AUC remains substantially above chance even at
$7\!\times\!7$ (59.2\,\% on COCO; 63.9\,\% on OpenImages), a resolution at which virtually
all semantic content is destroyed.  When the same experiment is repeated in greyscale, the
AUC approaches chance at $28\!\times\!28$, demonstrating that chromatic information is the
primary carrier of gender-correlated signal at low resolutions and that the model's
discriminative ability at very low colour resolutions cannot be attributed to residual shape
or object information.

To isolate this colour artifact in its simplest form, the authors train a logistic regression
classifier using only three features --- the mean pixel value of the red, green, and blue
colour channels --- and achieve AUCs of 58.0\,\% $\pm$\,0.4 and 59.1\,\% $\pm$\,0.4 on
COCO and OpenImages respectively.  This result is particularly striking: a model with access
to nothing more than the average colour of an image can distinguish between gender labels at
well above chance.  The likely cause is a confound in dataset composition: images of
male-labelled individuals are disproportionately situated in outdoor sports contexts with
dominant green tones (grassy fields), whereas images of female-labelled individuals more
frequently feature close-up portraits with lighter average pixel values.

\subsubsection*{Person and Background Artifacts}

The second experimental series uses a systematic occlusion protocol to disentangle gender
artifacts originating from the person versus those originating from the background.  The
following conditions are evaluated: the full unmodified image (\emph{Full}); the full image
with the background masked in black (\emph{Full NoBg}); the image with the person occluded
using their segmentation mask (\emph{MaskSegm}); the image with the person occluded using a
bounding box (\emph{MaskRect}); and combinations of these occlusions with and without the
background.

\paragraph{Person appearance.}
When the person pixels are fully visible, the gender artifact model achieves an AUC of
93.4\,\% $\pm$\,0.2 on COCO.  Removing the background reduces this only marginally to
92.7\,\% $\pm$\,0.2, confirming that appearance-level cues in the person are the dominant
signal under normal conditions.

\paragraph{Person shape and pose.}
Even when the person's appearance is entirely replaced by a white segmentation mask against
a visible background (MaskSegm), the model achieves 79.6\,\% $\pm$\,0.3 on COCO,
demonstrating that silhouette shape and outline alone --- which still reveal information such
as hair length and clothing profile --- carry substantial gendered information even before
texture or colour are accessible.  Removing the background as well (MaskSegm NoBg), the
model continues to classify at 74.8\,\% $\pm$\,0.3, confirming that person silhouette shape
and location are themselves meaningful gender artifacts independent of scene context.

The authors probe person shape further using the 17 COCO keypoint locations only, without
any pixel content.  This yields an AUC of 64.8\,\% $\pm$\,0.4, and a normalised pose-only
representation --- in which keypoints are rescaled to a fixed area and centred, removing
size and location information --- still achieves 58.0\,\% $\pm$\,0.0.  Qualitative
inspection of the highest and lowest scoring poses reveals an interpretable pattern:
male-associated poses tend to be smaller and active (e.g.\ sporting actions, jumping),
whereas female-associated poses tend to be larger relative to the frame and static.  This
is corroborated by a quantitative size analysis: the normalised area of female-labelled
persons in the COCO training set (0.18 $\pm$\, $10^{-4}$) is significantly larger than that
of male-labelled persons (0.13 $\pm$\, $10^{-5}$), a difference confirmed at the $p <
0.01$ level and replicated in OpenImages.

\paragraph{Background as gender artifact.}
Of equal importance for fairness applications is the finding that occluding the person while
leaving the background intact does not eliminate the gendered signal.  Under the MaskRect
condition --- which mirrors the occlusion strategy used by several prominent mitigation
methods~\cite{Hendricks_2018_WomenAlsoSnowboard,Wang_2019_BalancedDatasets} --- the AUC
remains at 70.8\,\% $\pm$\,0.3 on COCO and 63.1\,\% $\pm$\,0.4 on OpenImages.  Analogously,
even under MaskSegm (person occluded, background visible), the AUC is 79.6\,\%,
substantially higher than when both person and background are removed.  This demonstrates
that the background scene itself encodes gender-correlated information, a direct challenge to
the central assumption of fairness-through-blindness approaches.

Class Activation Map (CAM) analysis further illuminates the mechanism: when the person is
occluded, the model's attention shifts entirely onto contextual background objects, attending
to indoor objects (e.g.\ ovens and beds) to predict female labels and to outdoor objects
(e.g.\ skateboards and motorcycles) to predict male labels.  This confirms that background
scene type --- not merely abstract pixel statistics --- is the operative gender artifact in
these conditions.

Out-of-distribution evaluations (training on COCO and testing on OpenImages, and vice versa)
confirm that these artifact patterns generalise across datasets suggesting they reflect broad regularities in how photographic images are composed rather than idiosyncrasies of a single collection pipeline.

\subsubsection*{Contextual Object Artifacts}

The third component of the analysis addresses the gender information carried by the
categorical identity of objects present in a scene.  A logistic regression classifier is
trained on a binary object-presence vector (one dimension per annotated COCO or OpenImages
category), achieving an AUC of 75.4\,\% on COCO and 63.3\,\% on OpenImages.  The
classifier's weights reveal stereotypically gendered object associations: objects most
predictive of female-labelled images include hair driers, handbags, and teddy bears, while
those most predictive of male-labelled images include skateboards, ties, and snowboards.

An iterative removal experiment establishes that these associations are not concentrated in a
small number of salient objects.  Even when the 14 most gendered objects are retained and
all others removed, the classifier still achieves an AUC of 72.1\,\%.  Conversely, random
removal shows that 30 randomly selected objects from the 80 annotated COCO categories yield
66.8\,\%, and a classifier given only 10 randomly selected objects achieves 58.0\,\%.  This
demonstrates that gendered information is distributed broadly and redundantly across the
object vocabulary: there is no small set of ``gendered objects'' whose removal would approach
the elimination of object-level artifacts.

\subsubsection*{Analysis of Fairness-Through-Blindness Methods}

As a case study in how existing mitigation methods interact with these pervasive artifacts,
the authors apply the adversarial debiasing approach of Wang et
al.~\cite{Wang_2019_BalancedDatasets} to COCO images.  This method trains a U-Net on COCO
training images to identify and remove pixel regions corresponding to salient gender
artifacts.  The authors then examine which pixels are in practice removed from held-out
validation images.  The results are striking: 76.8\,\% of the removed pixels come from the
background rather than from the person, and 20.4\,\% $\pm$\,15.7 of all background pixels
across the dataset are ultimately masked.  The non-person objects most frequently affected
include giraffes, trains, elephants, buses, and horses --- objects that commonly co-occur
with people in active, outdoor scenes.

This demonstrates that the method effectively suppresses scene-level features --- including
objects that may be essential for downstream tasks such as object recognition --- rather than
targeting the gendered information embedded in the person's appearance as intended.  The
finding illustrates a more general trap in fairness-through-blindness approaches: because
gendered signals are distributed throughout the image rather than concentrated in the person,
any method optimising to remove them will inevitably also remove informationally relevant
scene content.

\subsubsection*{Discussion and Broader Implications}

The authors draw three broader conclusions from their findings.  First, because gender
artifacts are so widely distributed across image structure --- from mean colour to silhouette
shape to contextual object co-occurrence --- they cannot be removed without also removing
information that is relevant to the downstream task (e.g.\ objects and scenes), meaning that
fairness-through-blindness approaches inevitably degrade task utility.  Second, the
experiments point to an incoherence in the very concept of gender prediction from visual
data: if a model can predict gender labels from the mean pixel colour, from a 7$\times$7
blurred image, or from the presence of a skateboard, then what the model is actually
learning has little to do with gender expression in any socially meaningful sense and is more
likely to reflect the compositional biases of the collection process.  Third, the authors
recommend that practitioners shift towards \emph{fairness-aware} approaches --- methods that
explicitly acknowledge and account for distributional differences across demographic groups
rather than attempting to suppress them --- and towards disaggregated evaluation practices
that make such differences visible.
















\subsection{Skin Tone Annotation Frameworks}
The Fitzpatrick scale is the most widely used skin classification system in computer vision research. It was developed by Fitzpatrick \cite{Fitzpatrick_1988_SkinTypes} in clinical dermatology to describe how human skin responds to sun exposure, using six ordinal types ranging from Type I (very fair, always burns, never tans) to Type VI (deeply pigmented, never burns). It has been applied in machine learning fairness research on dermatological AI \cite{Groh_2021_Fitzpatrick17k} and in face analysis \cite{Buolamwini_2018_GenderShades}. However, the Fitzpatrick scale has several weaknesses for ML applications. Its definition is based on tanning behaviour rather than on the visual colour properties that cameras record, which creates a mismatch between what the scale was designed to measure and how it is used in image-based settings. The scale also places four of its six categories in the lighter range of skin tones, leaving only two categories to cover a wide range of darker tones. This means it has poor resolution at the darker end of the spectrum, where accurate distinction is most important for fairness analysis. Inter-rater reliability for Fitzpatrick typing from photographs is also lower than its clinical origins suggest, with notable disagreement reported particularly for Types III to V, where the visual boundaries between categories are unclear \cite{Groh_2021_Fitzpatrick17k}.

To address these shortcomings, Ellis Monk developed the Monk Skin Tone (MST) scale \cite{Schumann_2024_MonkSkinTone}, a ten-point ordinal scale designed specifically for machine learning and technology applications. The MST scale was created in collaboration with Google and has since been adopted as a standard for skin tone annotation by several major technology organisations. Compared to the Fitzpatrick scale, the MST scale distributes its categories more evenly across the full range of human skin tones, with better resolution at the darker end of the spectrum. It was validated through human perception studies that confirmed higher inter-rater reliability than the Fitzpatrick scale when applied to photographs, making it more suitable for image-based demographic annotation. The MST scale uses physical reference swatches, which allows both human annotators and automated systems to apply it consistently, giving it a clearer operational definition than the clinical Fitzpatrick categories.

A separate modelling decision concerns how skin tone categories are treated in statistical analysis. In an ordinal treatment, the rank ordering of categories is preserved but the intervals between them are not assumed to be equal. In a categorical treatment, each category is treated as an independent label with no ordering. In a grouped treatment, the full scale is collapsed into two or three broader categories to ensure sufficient sample sizes for analysis. The ordinal treatment is the most appropriate for both the Fitzpatrick and MST scales, given that skin tone varies continuously. The categorical treatment is less suitable because it ignores the natural ordering of the variable. The grouped treatment is common in practice because it provides the sample sizes needed for reliable statistical comparisons. The grouping choice matters directly for the correlation analysis in this thesis: the boundaries used determine whether skin-tone-related spurious features show up as detectable signals, and the choice must be guided by the class frequency distributions in the annotated image sets.

The choice of the MST scale over the Fitzpatrick scale for the skin tone annotation in this thesis is further supported by the findings of Heldreth et al. \cite{Heldreth_2024_WhichSkinToneMeasures}, who conducted a survey of 2,214 participants in the United States to compare perceived representativeness across three skin tone measures: the 6-point Fitzpatrick scale, the 40-point Fenty Beauty palette, and the 10-point Monk Skin Tone scale. Their results showed that the Fitzpatrick scale was perceived as significantly less representative than both the Monk and Fenty scales, and this difference was most pronounced among people with darker skin tones, Black participants, and women. Crucially, there was no statistically significant difference in perceived representativeness between the 10-point Monk scale and the 40-point Fenty palette, suggesting that the colour selection and gradation design of the MST scale achieves comparable inclusivity to a much finer-grained commercial palette without the cognitive burden and reduced inter-rater reliability that a 40-point annotation task would introduce. Heldreth et al. also found that white male participants perceived all three scales as equally representative, whereas all other demographic subgroups rated the Fitzpatrick scale as less inclusive. This asymmetry underscores the risk that relying on the Fitzpatrick scale for fairness evaluation may appear adequate from the perspective of the dominant demographic group while systematically underrepresenting the very populations whose treatment by AI systems is of greatest concern. These findings reinforce the decision to adopt the MST scale as the primary skin tone annotation framework in this thesis.

The practical advantages of a grouped treatment of the MST scale are empirically demonstrated by Mbatha et al. \cite{Mbatha_2024_SkinToneLighting}, who developed a CNN-based skin tone estimation model using the 10-point MST scale under diverse lighting conditions. Their study collected 21,375 images from 285 participants across the full pigmentation spectrum at Stellenbosch University, evaluating model accuracy under five light source types. When attempting fine-grained estimation on the full 10-point scale, their best regression model achieved 58.12\% accuracy at an estimated-to-target distance of 0.5. However, when the MST scale was segmented into three broader groups—light (tones 1–3), middle (tones 4–7), and dark (tones 8–10)—the model showed markedly different performance across groups, with strong accuracy for lighter tones (average LAB error of 3.67 ± 10.10), intermediate performance for darker tones (14.52 ± 11.58), and substantially lower accuracy for the middle range (22.47 ± 23.90). Using a threshold distance of 2 from the target tone, accuracy reached 97.16\% on average across all lighting conditions. These results indicate that a grouped treatment of the MST scale yields considerably more reliable annotation than fine-grained classification on the full 10-point scale, particularly when images are captured under variable lighting. This finding directly informed the decision in this thesis to adopt a grouped skin tone categorisation rather than attempting to classify images into all ten individual MST categories, as the grouped approach provides more robust statistical signals for correlation analysis while reducing the impact of annotation uncertainty at category boundaries.

A further methodological consideration for skin tone annotation is the choice of image region used for classification. Matias and Neto \cite{Matias_2024_EnhancingFairnessMST} investigated this question directly by comparing CNN models trained on full images against models trained on segmented facial skin regions, using a dataset of 39,605 images annotated according to the MST scale. Their experiments showed that while full-image models achieved higher raw accuracy (86.42\% for a DenseNet121 CNN), Grad-CAM visualisation analysis revealed that these models were learning to classify images based on background and contextual features rather than skin tone itself. The full-image CNN exploited correlations between specific dataset backgrounds and skin tone labels, effectively performing identity or context recognition rather than genuine skin tone generalisation. By contrast, models trained on segmented facial skin regions, where the background was removed and only the skin pixels of the face were retained, showed lower overall accuracy but produced Grad-CAM activations correctly focused on skin regions, indicating genuine skin tone feature learning. Their best segmented-skin model, a Random Forest classifier operating on colour histograms from the RGB, YCbCr, HSV, and LAB colour spaces, achieved 74\% accuracy and 92\% off-by-one accuracy on the 10-class MST scale. These results demonstrate that skin tone annotation from facial images should operate on segmented skin regions rather than full images to avoid learning spurious correlations with non-skin content. This insight guided the annotation approach adopted in this thesis, where skin tone classification is applied to detected facial regions rather than to the full image frame, reducing the risk that background content or photographic context biases the annotation output.
